\chapter{Classification of Lagrangian Fibrations over Closed Surfaces}\label{ch:classify-fibr}
\section{The Classification Problem}

Now that we have classified the integral affine structures on closed surfaces,
we can look to classify the Lagrangian fibrations $E \to B$ which induce a
given integral affine structure. The integral affine structure on the base $B$
is one invariant of a Lagrangian fibration however it is not the only one. We
have seen that there is a correspondence between integral affine structures on
$B$ and full rank lattices in $T^*B$ locally spanned by closed one-forms. This
lattice $\Lambda \subset T^*B$ gives rise to a Lagrangian fibration $\pi :
    T^*B/\Lambda \to B$ which is a canonical Lagrangian fibration we can associate
to $B$ once we have fixed the integral affine structure. There are in general,
other Lagrangian fibrations over $B$ which are in some sense \emph{twistings}
of this canonical fibration.

We wish to classify these fibrations up to fibrewise symplectomorphism,
covering the identity. We shall see that there are obstructions both to a
Lagrangian bundle over $B$ being topologically equivalent to this canonical
Lagrangian fibration $\pi : T^*B/\Lambda \to B$ as well as obstructions to a
Lagrangian bundle over $B$ being symplectically equivalent to the canonical
Lagrangian bundle. Since the problem of classifying these bundles will be an
issue of stitching together locally defined Lagrangian fibrations into a global
one, it will be helpful to do this in the language of sheaves and their
cohomology.

The idea behind sheaf cohomology is similar to that of group cohomology from
Chapter \ref{ch:prelim}. Sheaf cohomology will measure the failure of the
global sections functor to be exact. We will not develop this theory from
scratch in this thesis, but we refer the unfamiliar reader to
\cite{bredon1997sheaf}.

\section{Latticed one-forms}
We shall make use of the following sheaves on $B$.
\begin{definition}
    Let $B$ be a closed integral affine surface with period lattice $\Lambda \subset T^*B$.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\Lambda$ is the sheaf of sections of the period lattice $\Lambda \subset T^*B$.
        \item $\Lambda T^*B$ is the sheaf of Lagrangian sections of $T^*B$ and $T^*B$ is the sheaf of all sections of $T^*B$.
        \item $\mathcal{Z}(T^*B/\Lambda)$ is the sheaf of Lagrangian sections of $T^*B/\Lambda$ and $T^*B/\Lambda$ is the sheaf of all
              sections of $T^*B/\Lambda$.
        \item $\Lambda^2 B$ is the sheaf of closed $2$-forms on $B$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
    We note that we somewhat abuse notation and write the sheaf of sections of $T^*B,T^*B/\Lambda$ and $\Lambda$ by the same symbols. It will be obvious
    from the context whether we are considering these objects as bundles or as the sheaf of their corresponding sections.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark} \label{rem:differential-descends}
    Let $\Omega^1(B)$ denote the sheaf of differential forms on $B$. Then the differential
    $$
        d :\Omega^1(B) \to \Lambda^2(B)
    $$
    descends to a well-defined differential
    $$
        d_{\Lambda}: T^*B/\Lambda \to \Lambda^2(B)
    $$
    on the sections of $T^*B/\Lambda$ as sections of $\Lambda$ are given by closed one forms and hence vanish under this differential. This leads to the following notion of latticed forms.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Latticed one-form]
    Fix a period lattice $\Lambda \subset T^*B$. A \emph{latticed one-form} is a section of $T^*B/\Lambda$. A lattice one-form
    will be called closed if it determines a Lagrangian submanifold of the symplectic manifold $(T^*B/\Lambda, \tilde{\omega}_{can})$.
\end{definition}

A normal one-form on $B$ determines a latticed one-form by the bundle
projection $\pi: T^*B \to T^*B / \Lambda$. However, the converse is not true.
In general, there are obstructions to lifting a latticed one-form to a one-form
on $B$. There is a short exact sequence of sheaves $$ 0 \to \Lambda \to T^*B
    \to T^*B/ \Lambda \to 0 $$ and taking the long exact sequence in sheaf
cohomology we have $$ H^0(T^*B) \xrightarrow{d_1} H^0(T^*B/ \Lambda)
    \xrightarrow{d_2} H^1(\Lambda) \to \dots. $$ Since sheaf cohomology is a
derived functor of the global sections functor, $H^0(\cdot)$ consists of the
global sections over $B$.

A section of $T^*B/ \Lambda$, i.e. a latticed one-form, can be lifted to a
section of $T^*B$ if and only if it is in $\Im d_1$. By exactness, this is
equivalent to the latticed on-form being in $\ker d_2$. It follows, that if
$H^1(\Lambda) = 0$ then every latticed one-form lifts to a normal one-form on
$B$. This is true over contractible open sets as the sheaf cohomology of the
sheaf of sections of $\Lambda$ is a homotopy invariant - see Theorem
\ref{thm:homo-inv}.

Since the differential descends to $T^*B/\Lambda$, it makes sense to consider
elements in $H^2(B, \R)$ which arise from this differential.
\begin{definition}\label{def:coho-inv-}
    Let $s$ be a latticed one-form on $B$. Then the cohomology class $[ds]\in H^2(B, \R)$ will be called a \emph{cohomology invariant of the shift} $s$
\end{definition}

We now work towards showing when adding closed two-forms to $(T^*B/\Lambda,
    \tilde{\omega}_{can})$ produces equivalent Lagrangian bundles. This will turn
out to depend on the cohomological invariants of shifts.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:determines-shift}
    A fibrewise affine shift of $T^*B/\Lambda$ is determined by a latticed one-form and any latticed one-form determines such a shift. Moreover, this shift is a Lagrangian equivalence if the latticed one-form is closed.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
    Let $F: T^*B/\Lambda \to T^*B/\Lambda$ be a fibrewise affine . On each fibre, this takes the form $F_b(x) = x+c$ for some
    $c \in T^*_bB/\Lambda_b$ and hence is determined by a smooth section of $T^*B/\Lambda$.

    Conversely, given a section $\alpha: B \to T^*B/\Lambda$ we define an affine by
    $$ F_{\alpha} : T^*B/\Lambda \to T^*B/\Lambda $$ $$ x_b \mapsto x_b + \alpha_b.
    $$ Proposition \ref{prop:shift-by-closed} yields that this is a Lagrangian
    equivalence if and only if $\alpha$ is closed.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
    The contents of Theorem \ref{thm:cotangent-action} show that if $E \to B$ is a Lagrangian fibration with period lattice $\Lambda$, then a latticed one-form also
    determines a fibrewise  of $E$. Indeed, since over contractible open sets, $E \to B$ identifies with $T^*B/\Lambda \to B$, the converse can also be shown to be true.
\end{remark}

\begin{prop}\label{prop:equive-coho-shifts}
    Let $\rho: (E, \omega) \to B$ be a Lagrangian fibration with period lattice $\Lambda$. For $\alpha, \beta \in \Omega^2(B)$
    the bundles $(E, \omega + \rho^* \alpha)$ and $(E, \omega + \rho^* \beta)$ are Lagrangian equivalent if and only if
    $$[\alpha] - [\beta] = [d\eta] \in H^2(B)$$ where $[d\eta]$ is a cohomological invariant of the shift $\eta$.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
    Locally, our bundle $\rho: E \to B$ looks like the bundle $\pi:T^*B/ \Lambda \to B$. On overlaps $U \cap V$ where our bundle is of this form, the transition maps
    are given by Lagrangian equivalences of the bundle over $U \cap V$ and it is therefore enough to consider what happens on $T^*B/\Lambda \to B$.

    Proposition \ref{prop:shift-by-closed} implies that the bundles $(T^*B,
        \omega_{can} + \pi^* \alpha)$ and $(T^*B, \omega_{can} + \pi^* \beta)$ are
    Lagrangian equivalent if and only if $\alpha - \beta$ is an exact two-form. A
    latticed one-form is locally given by a usual one form, however a global lift
    may not exist.

    However, over contractible open sets, latticed one-forms are determined by
    usual one-forms. Hence if the shift by a latticed one-form $\eta$ is a
    Lagrangian equivalence of the bundles $(T^*B/\Lambda, \omega + \pi^* \alpha)$
    and $(T^*B/\Lambda, \omega + \pi^* \beta)$ then over contractible open sets we
    require that $\alpha - \beta = d \tilde{\eta}$ where $\tilde{\eta}$ is the lift
    of the latticed one-form to a normal one-form on $B$.

    Locally if $\alpha - \beta = d \tilde{\eta}$, then we can add any latticed
    one-form that lifts to a global one-form on $B$ to $\eta$ so that $\alpha -
        \beta = d (\tilde{\eta} + \varphi)$ which is still exact. Since these local
    lifts of $\eta$ glue together globally on $T^*B/\Lambda$, and we can modify
    $\eta$ by a global one-form $\varphi$, it follows that $[\alpha] - [\beta] =
        [d\eta] \in H^2(B, \R)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Calculating Cohomological Invariant of Shifts}
If we fix a period lattice $\Lambda$ and consider the bundle $T^*B/\Lambda$
then adding the pullback of a closed latticed one-form is a Lagrangian
equivalence. Proposition \ref{prop:equive-coho-shifts} tells us that the
possible Lagrangian equivalences of these bundles are determined by
cohomological invariants of a shift. We now wish to calculate the cohomological
invariants of shifts for the integral affine structures of Theorem
\ref{thm:classification-torus}. Since a class $[d\eta] \in H^2(\mathbb{T}^2,
    \R)$ is determined by the value $\int_{\mathbb{T}^2}d\eta$ we are left to
calculate the value of this integral. We note that this cohomology group is
spanned by the class of $[dx]$ and $[dy]$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:invar-calculation}
    We have that
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item For the lattice $\mathcal{A}_{a,b,c,d}$, the cohomological invariant of shifts
              are determined by integer covectors $\tilde{m} = kdx + rdy$ and $\tilde{n} =
                  ldx + sdy$ subject to no restrictions. Two invariants of shift are equivalent
              if the quantity $ka+lb+rc+sd$ is the same.
        \item  For the lattice $\mathcal{B}_{n,a,b}$, the cohomological invariant of shifts
              are determined by integer covectors $\tilde{m} = kdx + rdy$ and $\tilde{n} =
                  sdy$. Two invariants of shifts are equivalent if the quantity $ka+sb$ is the
              same.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    \begin{case}[$\mathcal{A}_{a,b,c,d}$]
        Recall that in this case the fundamental domain for the torus is generated by
        $$g = \left( 1, \begin{pmatrix}
                    a \\ c
                \end{pmatrix} \right) \text{ and } h=\left( 1, \begin{pmatrix}
                    b \\ d
                \end{pmatrix} \right)$$
        where the translational components are linearly independent. To construct a latticed one-form,
        we need the value of the one-form to agree with the identifications induced by the translational
        actions up to taking the quotient by the lattice $\Lambda \subset T^*B$. Namely, given the value
        of a latticed one-form $\alpha \in \Gamma(T^*B/\Lambda)$, if we fix a chart $U\subset \mathbb{T}^2$
        around $p$ and let $\alpha_p = \alpha_1dx + \alpha_2 dy \in T_p^*\mathbb{T}^2$, pulling back $\alpha$ by one
        of our generators $g$ or $h$ we should get back $\alpha_p + kdx + rdy$ for some integers $k,r \in \Z$
        as in affine coordinates $(x,y)$, the lattice $\Lambda$ is spanned by $dx$ and $dy$ over $\Z$.

        \begin{figure}[H]
            \centering
            \incfig[0.5]{DomainA}
            \caption{Fundamental domain for the first lattice.}
            \label{fig:first-lattice}
        \end{figure}

        The invariant we wish to calculate is $\int_{\mathbb{T}^2} d\alpha$ where
        $\alpha$ is a latticed one-form. By Stokes theorem, letting $U$ denote a
        fundamental domain for $\mathbb{T}^2$,
        \begin{align*}
            \int_{U} d\alpha & = \int_{\partial U} \alpha                                           \\
                             & = \int_A \alpha - \int_{A'}\alpha + \int_B \alpha - \int_{B'} \alpha
        \end{align*}
        where $A,A',B,B'$ are the boundaries of the fundamental domain depicted in Figure \ref{fig:first-lattice}.

        For example, translating $\alpha$ along $A$ by $g$ should return $\alpha$ up to
        some integer covector. In other words, we want $$ g^*\alpha = \alpha + \bar{m}
        $$ and $$ h^* \alpha = \alpha + \bar{n}. $$

        Denoting by $\bar{m} = (k,r)$ and $\bar{n} = (l,s)$, the integer covectors
        obtained from transporting along $A$ and $B$ respectively, we can then
        translate $g^*\alpha + \bar{m}$ by $h$ along $B'$. This should return the same
        one-form as first translating along $B$ and then translating along $A'$. For
        our first lattice, we see that this always holds true and so there is no
        restrictions on the covectors $\bar{m}$ and $\bar{n}$.

        Now we calculate the required integral. We note that on $A$ and $A'$, the one
        form $\alpha$ differs by $\bar{m}$ and so
        \begin{align*}
            \int_A \alpha - \int_{A'} \alpha & = \int_A \alpha  - \int_A \alpha  + \bar{m} \\
                                             & = - \int_A \bar{m}.
        \end{align*}
        Similarly, we have $\int_B \alpha - \int_{B'} = -\int_B \bar{n}$. Putting this together yields
        \begin{align*}
            \int_{U} d\alpha & = \int_A \alpha - \int_{A'} \alpha + \int_B \alpha - \int_{B'}                   \\
                             & = -\left(\int_A \bar{m} + \int_B \bar{n}\right)                                  \\
                             & = - \left(\int_{(0,0)}^{(a,c)} kdx + rdy + \int_{(0,0)}^{(b,d)} ldx + sdy\right) \\
                             & = -(ka + rc + lb + sd)
        \end{align*}
        with $k,l,r,s \in \Z$ which gives the required invariants.
    \end{case}

    \begin{case}[$\mathcal{B}_{n,a,b}$]
        Recall that for this family our fundamental domain is generated by
        $$g=\left( 1, \begin{pmatrix}
                    a \\ 0
                \end{pmatrix} \right) \text{ and } h=\left( \begin{pmatrix}
                    1 & n \\
                    0 & 1
                \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}
                    0 \\ b
                \end{pmatrix} \right).$$
        In this case, our integer covectors $\bar{m}$ and $\bar{n}$ are not arbitrary. We must have that
        \begin{align*}
            h^*(g^*\alpha + \bar{n}) + \bar{m} = g^*(h^*+\bar{m})+ \bar{n}
        \end{align*}
        and this implies that $h^*n = n$ or more algebraically
        \begin{align*}
            \begin{pmatrix}
                1 & n \\
                0 & 1
            \end{pmatrix}^T\begin{pmatrix}
                               l \\
                               s
                           \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
                                               l \\
                                               s
                                           \end{pmatrix}.
        \end{align*}
        This implies that $\bar{n} = \begin{pmatrix}
                0 \\ s
            \end{pmatrix}$ and this is the only restriction on our integer co-vectors $\bar{m}$ and $\bar{n}$. We now calculate the integral over
        our fundamental domain. We let $\alpha = \alpha_1(x,y) dx + \alpha_2(x,y)dy$. Again, applying Stokes theorem, we have that
        \begin{align*}
            \int_{\partial U} \alpha & = \int_A \alpha - \int_{A'} \alpha + \int_B \alpha - \int_{B'}\alpha                          \\
                                     & = \int_A \alpha - \int_A (h^*\alpha + \bar{m}) + \int_B \alpha - \int_B (g^*\alpha + \bar{n}) \\
                                     & = \int_A (\alpha - h^*\alpha - \bar{m}) - \int_B\bar{n}.
        \end{align*}
        Now, recalling that $h^* = \begin{pmatrix}
                1 & 0 \\
                n & 1
            \end{pmatrix}$ we have that
        \begin{align*}
            \alpha - h^*\alpha & = (\alpha_1(x,y) - \alpha_1(x,y))dx + n\alpha_1(x,y)dy \\
                               & = n\alpha_1(x,y)dy.
        \end{align*}
        Now, we have that
        \begin{align*}
            \int_A n\alpha_1(x,y)dy & = \int_{(0,0)}^{(a,0)} n\alpha_1(x,y)dy \\
                                    & =0
        \end{align*}
        as there is no $dx$ contribution. Hence, our invariant becomes
        \begin{align*}
            \int_{\partial U} \alpha & = -\left(\int_A \bar{m} + \int_B \bar{n}\right)                            \\
                                     & =-\left(\int_{(0,0)}^{(a,0)} \bar{m} + \int_{(0,0)}^{(0,b)} \bar{n}\right) \\
                                     & = -(ka + sb)
        \end{align*}
        where $k, s \in \Z$ and this completes the calculation of the cohomological invariants of shifts.
    \end{case}

    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \incfig[0.5]{DomainB}
        \caption{Fundamental domain for the second lattice.}
        \label{fig:second-lattice}
    \end{figure}
\end{proof}

\section{Luttinger Surgery}\label{sec:Lutt}
We will use Luttinger surgery in order to construct a Lagrangian fibration over
the torus or the Klein bottle with a given Chern class. The main idea is that
given a Lagrangian fibration with a fixed Chern class over one of these
surfaces, we can perform Luttinger surgery on it in order to obtain a bundle
with a global section. This corresponds to a Lagrangian fibration with the
trivial Chern class. By performing the surgery in the "opposite" direction i.e.
starting with a bundle with a global section and performing Luttinger surgery
on this, we will be able to produce a fibration with any given Chern class.

Luttinger surgery was first introduced in \cite{LuttingerSurgery} and provides
a way to modify a symplectic four-manifold $(M, \omega)$ along an embedded
Lagrangian tori $L \subset M$ such that the symplectic structure is preserved
and the geometry is altered only in a neighborhood of the torus. Topologically
it is equivalent to Dehn surgery with the framing chosen to be the Weinstein
framing. A framing is, roughly speaking, a parameterization of the tubular
neighborhood of the torus $L \subset M$. For a Lagrangian submanifold there is
a canonical choice of such a framing given by the Weinstein-Lagrangian tubular
neighborhood theorem.

\begin{theorem}[\cite{WEINSTEIN1971329}]\label{thm:weinstein-nbd}
    Let $ (M, \omega)$ be a symplectic manifold and $L \subset M$ be a Lagrangian submanifold. Then there
    exist tubular neighborhoods $U \subset M$ and $V \subset T^*L$ of $L$ together with a symplectomorphism:
    $$f : (U, \omega|_U ) \to (V, \omega_{\text{can}}|_V )$$
    with $f|_L = Id_L$.
\end{theorem}
The surgery construction works for general Lagrangian tori in a symplectic four-manifold but we will focus on the case
where our Lagrangian torus is given by a fibre of a Lagrangian fibration.

Let $B$ be the base space of such a Lagrangian fibration and let $T^*B/\Lambda
    \to B$ be the symplectic reference bundle. We view $B$ as the quotient
$\R^2/\langle A, B \rangle$ where $A,B$ are the generators of the affine
holonomy group. Let $p \in \R^2$ be in the interior of a fundamental domain for
the quotient and pick a small disc $D$ of radius $2\epsilon$ centered at this
point and a neighborhood $U$ of $D$. Over this neighborhood, our fibration
looks like $\pi : U \times T^2 \to U$. By Theorem \ref{thm:weinstein-nbd}, the
symplectic form on the tubular neighborhood $\pi^{-1}(D)$ of our Lagrangian
torus is inherited from the symplectic form on $T^*T^2$.

Now, if we take $\partial \pi^{-1}(D) = \partial (T^2 \times D)$ this is a
three torus $T^2 \times S^1$. If we remove the interior of this region from the
total space of the fibration, we are left with a manifold with boundary $T^3$.
The surgery now amounts to specifying an attaching map which glues together the
boundary of the removed $T^2 \times D$ back into the boundary created in the
total space of the fibration. This attaching map is given by a diffeomorphism
$F: T^3 \to T^3$.

Of course, so far this attaching map has not taken into account the symplectic
structure of the total space of the fibration. Indeed, the three torus has odd
dimension and hence it does not make sense to ask that the attaching map $F$
itself be a symplectomorphism. However, we can ask that $F$ is the restriction
of a symplectomorphism of the bundle $\pi^{-1}(N)$ where $N$ is a small annulus
around the boundary of the disc $\partial D$.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \incfig[0.5]{LuttingerSurgery}
    \caption{Performing Luttinger surgery on the Lagrangian torus $\pi^{-1}(p)$}
\end{figure}

Of course, one way to define such a map is to pick a Lagrangian section of
$\pi^{-1}(N)$ which is given by a closed one-form $\alpha$, and perform a shift
by this one-form. Then, by Proposition \ref{prop:shift-by-closed} this will be
a symplectomorphism and since it preserves the fibres, its restriction to
$\partial D$ will induce a map $F_{\alpha}: T^3 \to T^3$. A section of our
bundle over the boundary of the disc is a map $f: S^1 \to T^2$ and is
determined up to homotopy by an element of $\pi_1(T^2) = \Z^2$ as such a map is
a loop in $T^2$. The pair of integers $(m,n)$ represent the winding number of
such a map. Let $(x,y) = (r\cos \varphi, r \sin \varphi)$ be coordinates on $N$
centered at $p$. Then we can define a representative section for any $(m,n)$ to
be $$ s = \frac{1}{2\pi} (m\varphi dx + n \varphi dy).$$

One checks that after winding once around the circle i.e. $\varphi \mapsto
    \varphi + 2 \pi$, we have that our section changes by $mdx + ndy$ and hence
corresponds to the winding number given by $(m,n)$. This indeed defines a
section of the torus bundle since it is locally given by the symplectic
reference bundle and hence over $U$ looks like $T^*U/\langle dx, dy
    \rangle_{\Z}$.

In principle, one could perform the surgery by performing a shift using this
section. The issue is, this section is not Lagrangian. Indeed one calculates $$
    ds = \frac{1}{2 \pi}(m d \varphi \wedge dx + n d \varphi \wedge dy). $$ In
order to make this closed, one can add the correction term $$ \beta =
    \frac{1}{2\pi}(mx+ny)d\varphi $$ to $s$. It is easily checked that this does
not alter the homotopy class of the section $s$ as incrementing $\varphi$ by
$2\pi$ does not alter $\beta$. Hence, the section $F_{m,n} = s + \beta$
determines a symplectomorphism on a neighborhood of the bundle over $\partial
    D$ given by performing a shift by the closed one-form $F_{m,n}$ as in
Definition \ref{def:shift-by-form}. This completes the surgery construction.

\section{Chern Class and Lagrangian Class}
The strategy in classifying the Lagrangian bundles over $B$ will be to start
with the topological reference bundle $T^*B/\Lambda$ and using this, produce
new bundles by gluing these reference bundles together. We will then show that
we can essentially obtain any Lagrangian bundle over $B$ in this way. In
\cite{ActionAngle}, Duistermaat defined the \emph{Chern class} of a Lagrangian
fibration. It measures the obstruction for a Lagrangian fibration to be
topologically equivalent to $T^*B/\Lambda$. We now illustrate how this class is
defined.

We have seen previously that given a regular Lagrangian fibration with compact,
connected fibres, there is a canonical period lattice $\Lambda \subset T^*B$
and a fibrewise transitive action of $T^*B/\Lambda \to B$ on $E \to B$. This
endowed $E \to B$ with the structure of an \emph{affine} torus bundle, however
we could not yet identify it with the torus bundle $T^*B/\Lambda$. The
obstruction to topologically identifying these two fibrations is the
obstruction to $E \to B$ admitting a global section. We now answer the question
of when $E \to B$ admits a global section.

If we cover the base space $B$ with open sets $\{U_i\}$ such that each $U_i$ is
contractible, then, restricting the fibration to some $U_i$, all the
obstructions to this fibration admitting global action angle coordinates vanish
or in other words, locally this can be identified with $T^*B/\Lambda$. Hence,
if we pick our $U_i$ small enough we can choose sections $s_i: U_i \to E$.
Since the action of $T^*B/\Lambda$ on $E \to B$ is transitive, on the
intersections $U_i \cap U_j$, we know that $s_i = \mu_{ij} + s_j$ whereby
$\mu_{ij}$ is a section of $T^*B/\Lambda$ and $\mu_{ij} + s_j$ denotes the
abelian vector space action of this section on $s_j$. We now make the following
claim.

\begin{prop}\label{lemma:cocycle-chern}
    The sections $\mu_{ij}$ satisfy the cocycle condition and hence define a cohomology class
    $[\mu] \in H^1(T^*B/\Lambda)$. Moreover, the cohomology class does not depend on the choice of sections $s_i$.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
    We show that $\mu_{jk}+\mu_{ij} = \mu_{ik} $. We have that
    \begin{align*}
        \mu_{jk} + \mu_{ij} & =s_j-s_k + s_i-s_j \\
                            & = s_i -s_k
    \end{align*}
    and so the condition is immediate. Suppose we choose different sections $s_i',s_j'$. Since the $T^*B/\Lambda$ action is
    transitive we have
    $$s_i' = s_i + \sigma_i$$
    We now compute
    \begin{align*}
        \mu_{ij}' - \mu_{ij} & = s_i' - s_j' -s_i +s_j                      \\
                             & = s_i+ \sigma_i - s_j - \sigma_j - s_i + s_j \\
                             & = \sigma_i - \sigma_j
    \end{align*}
    and hence the difference is a Cech co-boundary and so is zero in cohomology as required.
\end{proof}

It is clear that if $\mu$ itself equals to zero, then we have a global section
of $M \to B$. Indeed if this is the case, then our sections are equal on the
overlaps and hence there is no problem gluing them together to obtain a global
section. However, this condition turns out to be in some sense too strong.
Indeed, if we loosen the condition to $[\mu] = 0 \in H^1(T^*B/\Lambda)$, it
turns out that we will be able to \emph{redefine} our sections so that they
equal on the overlap and hence glue together. The following Lemma proves this.

\begin{lemma}
    Suppose that $[\mu]=0 \in H^1(T^*B/\Lambda)$. Then there exists a global section of $M \to B$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    If $[\mu]=0$, then it follows that $[\mu]$ is a coboundary and so $\mu_{ij} = \sigma_i - \sigma_j$ where $\sigma_i : U_i \to T^*B/\Lambda$.
    We redefine our sections as
    $$
        s_i' = s_i - \sigma_i.
    $$
    Now, on intersections we have that
    \begin{align*}
        s_i' - s_j' & =  s_i - \sigma_i - s_j + \sigma_j    \\
                    & = s_i -  s_j - ( \sigma_i - \sigma_j) \\
                    & = s_i -  s_j - \mu_{ij}               \\
                    & = 0
    \end{align*}
    and hence we have no problem gluing our new local sections into a global section of $M \to B$.
\end{proof}

Hence, the obstruction the the existence of a global section of $M \to B$ lies
in the sheaf cohomology group $H^1(T^*B/\Lambda)$. In practice, this group may
not be so easy to calculate. We would hence like to relate this sheaf
cohomology group to a nicer cohomology group. Namely, we will be able to show
that there is an isomorphism $H^1(T^*B/\Lambda) \cong H^2(\Lambda)$. The sheaf
of sections of the period lattice $\Lambda$ is a locally constant sheaf as
there are local trivialisations of the period lattice such that it looks like
$U \times \Z^n$. Locally constant sheaves are equivalent to objects called
\emph{local systems}.

Calculating the cohomology of these local systems turns out to be much nicer
than calculating sheaf cohomology more generally. For example, it is possible
to compute this cohomology using cellular methods. Moreover, in the case that
the base manifold in question is an Eilenberg-MacLane $K(G, 1)$-space, we can
in fact use group cohomology to calculate this cohomology group which we shall
later see. For now, we shall look at how we can obtain this isomorphism.

\subsection{The Sheaf Theory Approach}
From our discussion in the previous section, the obstruction to $E \to B$
topologically identifying with $T^*B/\Lambda$ lived in $H^1(T^*B/\Lambda)$.
However, we noted that this may not be easy to calculate in general and so we
wished to relate it to a nicer cohomology group. We will see now how we can
make use of some standard sheaf theory to do this. A reference for some of the
standard sheaf cohomology results used here is \cite{bredon1997sheaf}.
\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:exact-seq-sheaf}
    The following diagram of sheaves is exact in all rows and columns.
    \[
        \begin{tikzcd}
            & & 0 \arrow[d] & 0 \arrow[d] & \\
            0 \arrow[r] & \Lambda \arrow[r, "i"] \arrow[d, equal]
            & \mathcal{Z}(T^*B) \arrow[r, "\mathrm{pr}"] \arrow[d]
            & \mathcal{Z}(T^*B / \Lambda) \arrow[r] \arrow[d, "j"]
            & 0 \\
            0 \arrow[r] & \Lambda \arrow[r]
            & T^*B \arrow[r, "\mathrm{pr}"] \arrow[d, "d"]
            & T^*B / \Lambda \arrow[r] \arrow[d, "d"]
            & 0 \\
            & & \Lambda^2 B \arrow[r, equal] \arrow[d]
            & \Lambda^2 B \arrow[d] \\
            & & 0 & 0
        \end{tikzcd}
    \]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    It is clear that the two horizontal sequences given by the inclusion and projection maps are exact. We show that
    $$
        0 \to \mathcal{Z}(T^*B) \to T^*B \xrightarrow{d} \Lambda^2 B \to 0
    $$
    is exact. The map $\mathcal{Z}(T^*B)  \to T^*B$ is the inclusion map and so is injective. It is also clear that the differential
    map $d$ is surjective onto the space of closed $2$-forms by definition. The kernel of the differential $d$ is the closed one-forms on $T^*B$ but these are
    given by Lagrangian sections. The other vertical sequence follows by the same argument and Remark \ref{rem:differential-descends}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
    The following two sheaf cohomological facts hold;
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $T^*B$ is a fine sheaf so that $H^i(T^*B) = 0$ for $i>0$.
        \item $H^i(\mathcal{Z}(T^*B)) \cong H^{i+1}(B, \R)$ for $i>0$
    \end{enumerate}

\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}

    That $T^*B$ is a fine sheaf amounts to the fact that smooth manifolds admit
    partitions of unity. Denoting by $C^{\infty}(B)$ the sheaf of smooth functions
    on $B$ this is a fine sheaf. We have a short exact sequence $$ 0 \to \R \to
        C^{\infty}(B) \to \mathcal{Z}(T^*B) \to 0 $$ where $\R \to C^{\infty}(B)$ is
    the inclusion and $C^{\infty}(B) \to \mathcal{Z}(T^*B) $ is the given by the
    differential. Since $df = 0$ if and only if $f$ is a constant function, this
    sequence is exact. Taking the associate long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology
    we obtain $$ \dots \to H^i(B, \R) \to H^i(C^{\infty}(B)) \to
        H^i(\mathcal{Z}(T^*B)) \to H^{i+1}(B, \R) \to \dots $$ Now since
    $C^{\infty}(B)$ is a fine sheaf, for $i>0$ we obtain $0\to
        H^i(\mathcal{Z}(T^*B) ) \to H^{i+1}(B, \R) \to 0$ giving the required
    isomorphism.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Chern Class}
We can now show the isomorphism $H^1(T^*B/\Lambda) \cong H^2(\Lambda)$ which
will lead to the definition of the Chern class.

\begin{theorem}
    There is an isomorphism $\delta: H^1(T^*B/\Lambda) \to H^2(\Lambda)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    We had the short exact sequence
    $$
        0 \to \Lambda \to T^*B \to T^*B / \Lambda \to 0
    $$
    from Lemma \ref{lemma:exact-seq-sheaf}. Taking the associated long exact sequence in cohomology yields
    $$
        \dots \to H^i(\Lambda) \to H^i(T^*B) \to H^i(T^*B/\Lambda) \to H^{i+1}(\Lambda) \to \dots
    $$
    Again, by Lemma \ref{lemma:exact-seq-sheaf}, we have that $H^i(T^*B) = 0$ for $i>0$ which gives an isomorphism $H^i(T^*B/\Lambda) \cong H^{i+1}(\Lambda)$ for $i>0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Chern Class]
    The image of $[\mu] \in H^1(T^*B/\Lambda)$ in $H^2(\Lambda)$ under $\delta$ is called the \emph{Chern class} and we shall denote it by $[\mu_C]$.
\end{definition}

The Chern class was defined by Duistermaat in \cite{ActionAngle}. The vanishing
of this class implies that the Lagrangian fibration $E \to B$ is topologically
equivalent to $T^*B/\Lambda \to B$. We note that this already depends on the
integral affine structure on $B$ as this determines our period lattice
$\Lambda$. It is a feature of the regularity of these Lagrangian torus
fibrations that such a structure exists. Indeed, if we allow our fibration to
have singular fibres, there is no longer a canonical symplectic reference
bundle to compare our fibration $E \to B$ with. Indeed, in this case one must
fix a so called \emph{reference system} for which to compare our Lagrangian
fibration to and then in a similar way one may define the Chern class of such a
singular fibration. More details on the singular case can be found in
\cite{zung2001symplectictopologyintegrablehamiltonian}.

The above discussion shows that given a regular Lagrangian torus fibration, we
can associate to it the invariant $[\mu_{C}] \in H^2(\Lambda)$. However, we
have not yet answered whether the converse of this statement holds true - that
is given a class $[\eta] \in H^2(\Lambda)$ can this be realised as the Chern
class of some Lagrangian torus fibration?

To answer this question, we consider the short exact sequence $$ 0 \to \Lambda
    \to \mathcal{Z} (T^*B) \to \mathcal{Z}(T^*B/\Lambda) \to 0 $$ and take the long
exact sequence in sheaf cohomology. This leads to the exact sequence $$ \dots
    \to H^1(\mathcal{Z}(T^*B/\Lambda)) \xrightarrow{\Delta} H^2(\Lambda)
    \xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}_{(B, \mathcal{A})}} H^2(\mathcal{Z} (T^*B)) \cong
    H^3(B, \R) \to \dots $$ The map $\mathcal{D}_{(B, \mathcal{A})}$ was considered
in \cite{dazord}.

They showed that a class $[\eta] \in H^2(\Lambda)$ is realisable as the Chern
class of a Lagrangian torus fibration if and only if $\mathcal{D}_{(B,
    \mathcal{A})}(\eta) = 0$. They proved this using methods from Poisson geometry.
One can show that Lagrangian torus fibrations are the same thing as complete
symplectic realisations of the Poisson zero structure on a manifold. In their
paper, they considered more generally symplectic realisations of regular
Poisson structures of which the zero Poisson structure is a special case.
Indeed there is a cohomology class determined by the characteristic foliation
$\mathcal{F}_{\pi}$ and they showed that under the map $\mathcal{D}_{(B,
    \mathcal{A})}$, the Chern class must map to this cohomology class. In the case
of the Poisson zero structure, the characteristic foliation of $(B, \pi\equiv
    0)$ consists of just the points of $B$ and this cohomology class will vanish.
For more information on Poisson geometry see the following sources
\cite{CrainicFernandesMarcut2021}.

\subsection{The Lagrangian Class}
This answers the question of which $[\eta] \in H^2(\Lambda)$ can arise as the
Chern class of some Lagrangian fibration. However, this invariant is only
topologically sharp. Once we have fixed an integral affine manifold $B$ and a
Chern class $[\mu_{C}] \in H^2(\Lambda)$, we can have Lagrangian fibrations
over $B$ with the same Chern class $[\mu_C]$ but which are not Lagrangian
equivalent to each other. If we recall how the Chern class was constructed this
fact should not be surprising. Indeed, we did not take into account the
symplectic structure of our Lagrangian fibration in the construction of our
Chern class since we allowed the sections of $T^*B/\Lambda$ to be arbitrary so
it would be too much to hope for that we would achieve a symplectic
classification. We can fix this problem by choosing our sections to be sections
of $\mathcal{Z}(T^*B/\Lambda)$ as we shall now see.

\begin{definition}[\cite{zung2001symplectictopologyintegrablehamiltonian}]
    Let $M \to B$ be a Lagrangian fibration with period lattice $\Lambda$. Let $\{U_i\}_{i \in \mathbb{N}}$ be a trivializing cover and let
    $s_i: U_i \to \mathcal{Z}(T^*B/\Lambda)$. Then $\mu_{ij} = s_i -s_j$ defines a one-cocycle independent of the choice of section and hence a
    cohomology class $[\mu] \in H^1(\mathcal{Z}(T^*B/\Lambda))$. The class $[\mu]$ is called the \emph{Lagrangian class} of $M \to B$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
    The fact that the class $[\mu] \in H^1(\mathcal{Z}(T^*B/\Lambda))$ is well-defined and independent of the choice of sections follows from the same arguments as in Proposition \ref{lemma:cocycle-chern}.
\end{remark}

If the class $[\mu] = 0$ then we can obtain a global \emph{Lagrangian} section
of $M \to B$ and hence we obtain that $M \to B$ is Lagrangian equivalent to
$T^*B/\Lambda \to B$. In fact, the following proposition shows that every
element of this cohomology group will be the Lagrangian class of some
Lagrangian torus fibration.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:every:lagrangian-class}
    Every class $[\mu] \in H^1(\mathcal{Z}(T^*B/\Lambda))$ can be realised as the Lagrangian class of some Lagrangian torus fibration.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
    Suppose we are given some $[\mu] \in \mathcal{Z}(T^*B/\Lambda)$. Then picking a good cover $\{U_i\}_{i \in \mathbb{N}}$ of $B$ we may assume $[\mu]$
    can be represented by a one-cocycle $\alpha_{ij} : U_i \cap U_j \to \mathcal{Z}(T^*B/\Lambda)$. Now, each $\alpha_{ij}$ determines a fibrewise affine
    $$
        s_{\alpha_{ij}}: T^*(U_i \cap U_j)/\Lambda\to T^*(U_i \cap U_j)/\Lambda
    $$
    $$
        (b, m) \mapsto (b, m + \alpha_{ij}(b))
    $$
    where the additive action of $\alpha_{ij}$ comes from the action of $T^*B$ on $M \to B$. On triples $U_i \cap U_j \cap U_j$ we require that
    transitioning a point in the fibre by $s_{\alpha_{ij}} \circ s_{\alpha_{jk}} = s_{\alpha_{ik}}$ but this holds precisely because $\alpha_{ij}$ satisfies the cocyle condition.
    By Proposition \ref{prop:shift-by-closed}, the  $s_{\alpha_{ij}}$ is a symplectomorphism
    of the Lagrangian fibration $T^*(U_i \cap U_j)/\Lambda$ as $\alpha$ is closed. Hence, the transition maps are symplectomorphisms and so our local forms glue together
    to form a global symplectic form on $M$ and this becomes a Lagrangian fibration as locally in each chart $U_i$ it is by construction a Lagrangian fibration.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    In fact,
    taking the long exact sequence in cohomology of the first row of Lemma
    \ref{lemma:exact-seq-sheaf}, we obtain exactness at
    $H^1(\mathcal{Z}(T^*B/\Lambda)) \xrightarrow{\Delta} H^2(\Lambda)$ and hence
    every class $\Delta([\mu]) \in H^2(\Lambda)$ lies in the kernel of
    $\mathcal{D}_{(B, \mathcal{A})}$ the Dazord-Delzant homomorphism and hence can
    be realised as a Lagrangian torus fibration. However, this alone tells us only that $\Delta([\mu])$ arises
    as the Chern class of a Lagrangian torus fibration.
\end{remark}

The following theorem summarizes the discussion from this section so far.

\begin{theorem}
    Let $B$ be a smooth manifold. The regular Lagrangian torus fibrations over $B$ are determined by
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The integral affine structure on $B$. This determines a symplectic reference
              bundle $T^*B/\Lambda$.
        \item Fixing the integral affine structure on $B$, the Lagrangian fibrations over $B$
              are topologically determined by a Chern class $[\mu_{C}] \in H^2(\Lambda)$. The
              Chern class determines the topological type of the Lagrangian torus fibration
              and its vanishing implies the fibration is topologically equivalent to
              $T^*B/\Lambda$. An element $[\mu_{C}] \in H^2(\Lambda)$ is the Chern class of a
              Lagrangian torus fibration if and only if $\mathcal{D}_{(B,
                  \mathcal{A})}([\mu_C]) = 0$ where $\mathcal{D}_{(B, \mathcal{A})}$ is the
              Delzant-Dazord homomorphism.
        \item The obstruction to our bundle being symplectically equivalent to $T^*B/\Lambda$
              is given by the Lagrangian class $[\mu] \in H^1(\mathcal{Z}(T^*B/\Lambda))$.
              Every Lagrangian class arises as the Lagrangian class of some Lagrangian torus
              fibration.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

In the case of an integral affine surface, we can make some of this discussion
a bit more explicit. We have seen earlier that an element $[\mu]\in
    H^2(\Lambda)$ is the Chern class of a Lagrangian torus fibration if and only if
it vanished under the Delzant-Dazord homomorphism. In the case of integral
affine surfaces, it turns out that every class $[\mu] \in H^2(\Lambda)$ can be
realised as the Chern class of a Lagrangian torus fibration.

There is a short exact sequence

$$0 \to \Lambda \to \mathcal{Z}(T^*B) \to \mathcal{Z}(T^*B/\Lambda) \to 0 $$
from which we obtain the long exact sequence in cohomology
\begin{align}\label{eq:exact-seq}
    \dots \to H^1(\Lambda) \to H^1(\mathcal{Z}(T^*B)) \cong H^2(B, \R) & \xrightarrow{d} H^1(\mathcal{Z}(T^*B/\Lambda)) \xrightarrow{\Delta} H^2(\Lambda) \\
                                                                       & \to H^1(\mathcal{Z}(T^*B)) \cong H^3(B, \R)=0 \notag
\end{align}
where the isomorphisms $H^i(\mathcal{Z}(T^*B)) \cong H^{i+1}(B, \R)$ are from Lemma \ref{lemma:exact-seq-sheaf}. By exactness this yields that
$H^2(\Lambda) \cong \dfrac{H^1(\mathcal{Z}(T^*B/\Lambda))}{\ker \Delta}$. By Proposition \ref{prop:every:lagrangian-class}, every element of $\dfrac{H^1(\mathcal{Z}(T^*B/\Lambda))}{\ker \Delta}$ arises
as the Lagrangian class of a Lagrangian torus fibration and hence, as this group is isomorphic to $H^2(\Lambda)$ every class $[\mu] \in H^2(\Lambda)$ can be realised as the Chern class of a Lagrangian torus fibration.

\begin{remark}\label{prop:classifying-group}
    Once we have fixed a Chern class we can ask which Lagrangian classes map to this Chern class. The long exact sequence gives us a map $j: H^1(\Lambda) \to H^2(B,\R)$.
    The group $\ker \Delta = \dfrac{H^2(B, \R)}{\text{Im } j}$ paramaterizes the Lagrangian classes $[\mu]$ which map to a fixed Chern class
    $[\mu_C]$. These correspond to Lagrangian fibrations that are topologically equivalent but not symplectically equivalent.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
    Exactness of the long exact sequence \ref{eq:exact-seq} shows that the group $\ker \Delta = \dfrac{H^2(B, \R)}{\text{Im } j}$.
\end{remark}

\begin{prop}\label{prop:generated-shifts}
    The group $\text{Im } H^1(\Lambda) \subset H^2(B, \R)$ is generated by the cohomological invariants of shift from Definition \ref{def:coho-inv-}.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
    Applying the long exact
    sequence in cohomology to the second row of Lemma \ref{lemma:exact-seq-sheaf}, we obtain that
    $$
        H^0(T^*B/\Lambda) \xrightarrow{\delta} H^1(\Lambda) \to H^1(T^*B) =0
    $$
    and so $\delta$ is surjective. We therefore have that $j(H^1(\Lambda)) \subset H^2(B,\R)$ is the same
    as $j \circ \delta(H^0(T^*B/\Lambda)) \subset H^2(\Lambda)$ where $j$ is the map
    $j: H^1(\Lambda) \to H^2(B, \R)$ from Remark \ref{prop:classifying-group}.

    It remains to show that $j \circ \delta$ coincides with the differential
    $\tilde{d}_{\Lambda}: H^0(T^*B/\Lambda) \to H^2(B, \R)$ since by definition
    $H^0(T^*B/\Lambda)$ is the space of latticed one-forms. This follows from
    another version of $H^1(\mathcal{Z}(T^*B/\Lambda)) \cong H^2(B, \R)$. Again,
    applying the long exact sequence in cohomology to the short exact sequence $$ 0
        \to \mathcal{Z} (T^*B) \to T^*B \to \Lambda^2 B \to 0 $$ yields the long exact
    sequence $$ 0 \to H^0(\mathcal{Z} (T^*B)) \to H^0(T^*B) \xrightarrow{d}
        H^0(\Lambda^2 B) \to H^1(\mathcal{Z} (T^*B)) \to 0 $$ since $T^*B$ is a fine
    sheaf. This gives that $$ H^1(\mathcal{Z} (T^*B)) \cong \dfrac{H^0(\Lambda^2
            B)}{dH^0(T^*B)} $$ and this is the de Rham cohomology group $H^2(B, \R)$ by
    definition.

    Now, we have the following short exact sequence
    \[
        \begin{tikzcd}
            0 \arrow[r] & \mathcal{Z}(T^*B/\Lambda) \arrow[r]
            & T^*B / \Lambda  \arrow[r]
            & \Lambda^2 B \arrow[r]
            & 0
        \end{tikzcd}
    \]
    and from the long exact sequence in cohomology, there is a map $d_{\lambda}:
        H^0(T^*B/\Lambda) \to H^0(\Lambda^2 B)$. The required result now follows from
    commutativity of the following square.
    \[
        \begin{tikzcd}
            H^0(T^*B/\Lambda) \arrow[r, "d_{\Lambda}"] \arrow[d, "\delta"] & H^0(\Lambda^2 B) \arrow[d, "\pi"] \\
            H^1(\Lambda) \arrow[r, "j"] & H^2(B, \R)
        \end{tikzcd}
    \]
    where $\pi \circ d_{\Lambda} = \tilde{d}_{\Lambda}$ is the required
    differential.
\end{proof}

\section{Computing the Chern Class}
After the discussion in the previous section, our strategy will be to compute
all of the possible Chern classes for our integral affine closed surfaces. Once
we have fixed a Chern class, we can then look at the possible Lagrangian
classes that map to it. A lot of the complexity in this process will be in the
calculation of the sheaf cohomology groups. Fortunately, since the sheaf of
sections of the period lattice $\Lambda$ is a locally constant sheaf, its sheaf
cohomology will be equal to the cohomology of the so called \emph{local system}
it determines.

We shall now recall some basic facts and results around the theory of local
systems. Some references on the theory of local systems include
\cite{ElZeinSnoussi2009}, \cite{whitehead1978elements} and
\cite{Davis2001LectureNI}.

\subsection{Local Systems}
Recall that a constant sheaf $\mathcal{F}$ over a topological space $X$, is a
sheaf such that for each open set $U \subset X$ we have that $\mathcal{F}(U) =
    R$ for some fixed group/ring/module $R$.

\begin{definition}[Local system]
    A local system on a topological space $X$, is a locally constant sheaf $\mathcal{F}$ - e.g. for each $x \in X$, there is a neighborhood $U \subset X$ such that
    for all $V \subseteq U$ we have that $\mathcal{F}|_V$ is a constant sheaf for some fixed group/ring/module $R$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
    For the remainder of this discussion we will take $R$ to be an abelian group although most of this holds in greater generality.
\end{remark}

A key feature of these local systems is that if our topological space is nice
enough, for example a manifold $M$, then the local systems over $M$ are in
bijection with representations of the fundamental group. In fact, an even
stronger condition than this holds. It turns out that this bijection can be
upgraded to an equivalence of categories and hence the study of local systems
over these topological spaces is equivalent to studying the representations of
its fundamental group.

Indeed, given a local system $\mathcal{F}$ over $M$ with fixed module a finite
dimensional vector space $V$, one can associate an "algebraic" monodromy to it.
Namely, given $[\gamma] \in \pi_1(M,x)$ we can associate an automorphism of the
stalk $\mathcal{F}_x \cong R$. In this way we obtain a monodromy map $$ \rho :
    \pi_1(M, x) \to \text{Aut}(\mathcal{F}_x) $$ and this is how we associate a
representation of the fundamental group to the local system.

Conversely, given a representation $\rho : \pi_1(M, x) \to \text{Aut}(R)$ we
can associate to it a locally constant sheaf as follows. Let $\pi: \tilde{M}
    \to M$ be the universal cover of $M$ and denote by $V_{\tilde{M}}$ the constant
sheaf on $\tilde{M}$. Then we define the sections $\mathcal{F}(U)$ to be
sections of $V_{\tilde{M}}$ over $\pi^{-1}(U)$ that are equivariant with
respect to the action of the fundamental group $\pi_1(M,x)$ which acts on
$\tilde{M}$ by deck transformations. This determines a locally constant sheaf
as if we pick a small enough neighborhood $U \subset M$ that is evenly covered,
then the sheaf is constant on $U$ as $\pi|_{U_{\gamma}}$ is a homeomorphism and
$\pi \circ \gamma = \pi$ where $\gamma$ is a deck transformation. For more
details see \cite{ElZeinSnoussi2009}.

\subsection{Cohomology of Local Systems}
We could calculate the cohomology of a local system by calculating its sheaf
cohomology directly. However, there turns out to be a nicer way to calculate
the cohomology in this case which is amenable to cellular methods.

In fact, the equivalence between local systems and representations of the
fundamental group leads to two different ways of calculating the cohomology of
the local system. We now briefly illustrate how to calculate through the two
different perspectives.

\subsubsection{The singular cohomology approach}
The first approach we take is the singular cohomology approach. Given a
representation $\rho: \pi_1(M) \to \text{Aut}(R)$ our construction of a local
system in fact give a fibre bundle $E$ with fibre $R$ by $$ E:= (\tilde{M}
    \times R)/\pi_1(M) $$ where $\gamma \cdot (\tilde{m}, v) = (\gamma \cdot
    \tilde{x}, \rho(\gamma)v)$ see \cite{Davis2001LectureNI}. Let $\sigma: \Delta^n
    \to M$ be a singular $k$-simplex in $M$ and denote by $e_0 = (1, 0, \dots , 0)$
the base point of $\sigma$ and $E_{\sigma(e_0)}$ the fibre above $\sigma(e_0)
    \in X$. We let $S^k(M; E)$ the set of formal sums of functions $f$ that assign
to each singular simplex $\sigma: \Delta^k \to M$, an element $f(\sigma) \in
    E_{\sigma(e_0)}$. Then $S^k(M; E)$ is an abelian group and we can associate a
coboundary operator to it by $$ \delta : S^k(M;E) \to S^{k+1}(M;E) $$ given by
$$\delta(f)(\sigma) = (-1)^k \left(\gamma_{\sigma}^{-1}(f(\partial_0 \sigma)) +
    \sum_{i=1}^{k+1}(-1)^ic(\partial_i \sigma)\right)$$ where the $\partial_i$ are
the usual boundary maps for singular simplices and $$ \gamma_{\sigma}:
    E_{\sigma(0,1,0,\dots , 0)} \to E_{\sigma(1,0,0,\dots , 0)} $$ is an
isomorphism induced by the path $$\tilde{\gamma}_{\sigma}: [0,1] \to M$$ $$ t
    \mapsto \sigma(t, 1-t, 0 \dots , 0)$$ via path lifting as $\rho: E \to M$ is a
covering space.

A computation shows that $\delta^2=0$ and we will denote the corresponding
cohomology by $H_{\text{sing}}^*(M, R)$ where it is understood that $R$ is
viewed as a $\pi_1(M)$-module through the action of the representation.

\begin{definition}\label{def:singular-coho-localsystem}
    Let $\rho:\pi_1(M) \to \text{Aut}(R)$ determine a local system. The \emph{singular cohomology of the local system} is the cohomology of the complex $(S^K(M;E), \delta)$
\end{definition}

\subsubsection{The equivariant or Borel cohomology approach}
Equivariant or Borel cohomology was introduced by Borel in \cite{Borel} and
encodes information about group actions on a topological space $X$. It has
connections to Lie groupoid cohomology and has found applications throughout
algebraic geometry and differential geometry. The description that we now give
of the cohomology of a local system is a special case of this equivariant
cohomology.

We have that there is a right action of the fundamental group by deck
transformations on the universal cover $\tilde{M}$ of our manifold $M$.
Denoting by $C^*(\tilde{M})$ the free abelian group of simplices $\sigma:
    \Delta^n \to \tilde{M}$, this action turns $C^*(\tilde{M})$ into a right
$\pi_1(M)$-module. Namely, given a simplex and $\gamma \in \pi_1(M)$ we can
define this right action as $\sigma \cdot \gamma = \gamma \star \sigma $ where
$\gamma \star \sigma $ is the action of $\gamma$ by deck transformations and
extend to the group ring by linearity. Now, we have that the fixed group of our
local system $R$ is a right $\pi_1(M)$-module via the representation $\rho:
    \pi_1(M) \to \text{Aut}(R)$. We would like to apply
$\text{Hom}_{\Z[\pi_1(M)]}(-,R)$ to our chain complex of right
$\Z[\pi_1(M)]$-modules $C^*(\tilde{M})$ but we cannot do so as
$\text{Hom}_{\Z[\pi_1(M)]}(-,-)$ must take in either two right modules or two
left modules. However, we can solve this problem by turning $C^*(\tilde{M})$
into a left $\Z[\pi_1(M)]$-module by defining the action of $g \in
    \Z[\pi_1(M)]$ to be $g \cdot z = z \cdot g^{-1}$. Applying
$\text{Hom}_{\Z[\pi_1(M)]}(-,R)$ yields a co-complex.

\begin{definition}\label{def:equivariant-coho}
    The cohomology of this complex is called the \emph{cohomology of M with twisted coefficients} and is
    the same as the equivariant cohomology of the universal cover $\tilde{M}$. Fixing a representation $\rho: \pi_1(M) \to \text{Aut}(R)$ we shall
    denote this cohomology as $H^i(M, R_{\rho})$.
\end{definition}

The two approaches described can be seen to be equivalent. We shall not give
the proof of this but it can be found in Theorem 3.5 of
\cite{whitehead1978elements}.

\subsection{Relation to group cohomology}
There is yet another approach to be taken in this case that our manifold is a
"nice" space by which we mean it is an Eilenber Mac-Lane space of type $K(G,1)$
as of Definition \ref{def:eilenberg-mac}.

A local system is determined by a representation of the fundamental group on an
abelian group $R$ and hence, one might speculate that the group cohomology of
$H^i(\pi_1(M) ,R)$ where we view $R$ as a $\pi_1(M)$-module is the same as the
cohomology of the local system determined by it. However, this is not the case
in general although there are relationships between the two. The following
lemma is a special case of when the two coincide.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:equiv_localsystem-group}
    Let $M$ be an Eilenberg-MacLane space of type $K(G,1)$ and let $\pi_1(M) \to \text{Aut}(R)$ be a representation. Then the
    group cohomology $H^i(\pi_1(M), R)$ and the cohomology of the local system coincide.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    We will give the proof in the case of $M$ being a CW-complex although this is not strictly needed \cite{whitehead1978elements}. For $n\geq 2$ we have that $S^n$ is simply connected and so given a map $f: S^n \to M$ there is a unique lift to the universal cover.
    \[
        \begin{tikzcd}
            & \widetilde{M} \arrow[d, "\pi"] \\
            S^n \arrow[ru, dashed, "\widetilde{f}"] \arrow[r, "f"] & M
        \end{tikzcd}
    \]
    and hence we get a map $$ \varphi : \pi_n(M) \to \pi_n(\tilde{M}) $$ $$ [f]
        \mapsto [\widetilde{f}] $$ The covering map also gives a map in the other
    direction and these maps are easily seen to be inverses of one another. This
    leads to an isomorphism $\pi_n(M) \cong \pi_n(\tilde{M})$ for $n \geq 2$.

    Hence, if $M$ is an Eilenberg-MacLane space of type $K(G,1)$ then its universal
    cover has $\pi_n(\tilde{M})=0$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Now if we consider
    the chain complex

    $$
        \dots \to C^2(\tilde{M}) \to C^1(\tilde{M}) \to C^0(\tilde{M}) \xrightarrow{\epsilon} \Z \to 0
    $$

    where $\epsilon$ is the augmentation map. By Whiteheads lemma (Corollary 7.34
    \cite{Davis2001LectureNI}), $\tilde{M}$ is homotopy equivalent to a point and
    so $H^i(C^*(M)) = 0$ for $i>0$ and $H^0(C^*(M)) = 0$. Hence the above sequence
    is exact and provides a free resolution of $\Z$ by $\pi_1(M)$-modules and this
    computes the group cohomology after applying the $\text{Hom}_{\Z[\pi_1(M)]}(-,
        R)$ functor and taking the cohomology of the resulting complex.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    If $M$ is a $K(G,1)$ space and path connected, then $M$ is often called \emph{aspherical}.
\end{remark}

We now make the following observation which we shall use when computing the
Chern class of a Lagrangian torus fibration over a complete integral affine
manifold.

\begin{lemma}
    Let $M$ be a complete affine manifold. Then $M$ is aspherical and hence the cohomology of the local system determined by
    its linear holonomy representation, is equal to the group cohomology of $\pi_1(M)$ with respect to the linear holonomy representation.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Since $M$ is complete, the developing map $\dev: \tilde{M} \to \R^n$ is a diffeomorphism. Since $\R^n$ is contractible it follows that $\tilde{M}$ is also and hence by
    Lemma \ref{lemma:equiv_localsystem-group}, the group cohomology and the cohomology of the local system coincide.
\end{proof}

In the case of the Chern class, this lived in $H^2(\Lambda)$ where $\Lambda$ is
the locally constant sheaf of sections of the period lattice. Our previous
discussion shows that in the case of a complete integral affine manifold with
linear holonomy representation $\text{Lin}\circ \hol: \pi_1(M) \to
    \text{Aut}(\Z^n) $, the sheaf cohomology of $\Lambda$ coincides with the group
cohomology of $\pi_1(M)$ with respect to this linear holonomy representation
once one verifies that these local systems do indeed coincide. In group
cohomology, the group $H^2$ classifies objects called \emph{group extensions}.
We quickly recap these now.

\begin{definition}
    A \emph{group extension} of $G$ by $A$ is a short exact sequence of groups
    $$
        0 \to A \to E \xrightarrow{\pi} G \to 1
    $$
    where $A$ is abelian.
\end{definition}

There is a notion of equivalence for group extensions.
\begin{definition}
    An equivalence of extensions is a group
    isomorphism $\varphi : E \to E'$ such that the following diagram commutes:
    \[
        \begin{tikzcd}
            0 \arrow[r] & A \arrow[r] \arrow[d, equal] & E \arrow[r, "\pi"] \arrow[d, "\varphi"] & G \arrow[r] \arrow[d, equal] & 1 \\
            0 \arrow[r] & A\arrow[r] & E' \arrow[r, "\pi'"] & G \arrow[r] & 1.
        \end{tikzcd}
    \]
\end{definition}

Given a group extension of $G$ by $A$ then there is a unique $G$-module
structure induced on $A$. Equivalent group extensions induce the same
$G$-module structure on $A$. Fixing a $G$-module $A$, there may be multiple
extensions that induce the given $G$-module structure. We have the following
classification result.

\begin{theorem}[\cite{brown2012cohomology}]
    Fix a $G$-module $A$. Let $\epsilon(G, A)$ denote the set of extensions which induce the given
    $G$-module structure on $A$. Then we have that there is a bijection
    $$
        H^2(G, A) \iff \epsilon(G, A).
    $$
\end{theorem}

This establishes the following perspective on the Chern classes of a Lagrangian
fibration over a closed integral affine surface.

\begin{prop}
    Let $M$ be a closed integral affine surface. Then there is a one-to-one correspondence between
    Lagrangian fibrations over $M$ with a given Chern class and isomorphism classes of group extensions
    $$
        0 \to \Z^n \to E \to \pi_1(M) \to 1
    $$
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
    This follows from the fact that every Chern class of an integral affine closed surface is realisable as the Chern class of a Lagrangian fibration and the bijection established in the previous theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    Under this correspondence, the symplectic reference fibration corresponds to the group extension class of
    $$0 \to \Z^n \to \Z^n \rtimes \pi_1(M) \to \pi_1(M) \to 1$$
\end{remark}

What remains to show in this section, is that calculating the cohomology of the
local system in the sense of the two different definitions given, is equivalent
to calculating the sheaf cohomology of the locally constant sheaf. We will not
give the proof but we will outline where one can find the required equivalence.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:equiv-cohomology-theories}
    Let $M$ a manifold and let $\mathcal{F}$ be a local system over $M$ with fixed abelian group $R$. Then the following cohomology theories are equivalent.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The sheaf cohomology of the locally constant sheaf $\mathcal{F}$ over $M$.
        \item The singular cohomology of the local system as in Definition
              \ref{def:singular-coho-localsystem}.
        \item The cohomology of $M$ with twisted coefficients as in Definition
              \ref{def:equivariant-coho}.
        \item The Cech cohomology of the locally constant sheaf $\mathcal{F}$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    For $1) \iff 2)$ see Theorem 1.1 in Chapter 3 of \cite{bredon1997sheaf}. For $2) \iff 3)$ see Theorem $3.4$ of \cite{whitehead1978elements}.
    For $4) \iff 1)$ see Theorem $4.13$ of \cite{bredon1997sheaf}.
\end{proof}

We also note the following important fact about the homotopy invariance of the
cohomology of locally constant sheaves.

\begin{theorem}[Theorem 21.5. \cite{dahlhausen2020algebraTopologyII}]\label{thm:homo-inv}
    Let $f: M \to N$ be a homotopy equivalence. Then for every locally constant sheaf $\mathcal{F}$ on $Y$, we have that
    $$
        H^*(Y, \mathcal{F}) \cong H^*(X, f^{-1}\mathcal{F}).
    $$
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Classifying Lagrangian fibrations over $S^1$}
Before we give the classification of regular Lagrangian torus fibrations over
compact surfaces, we first give the classification over $S^1$ for completeness.
\begin{lemma}
    The Lagrangian fibrations over $S^1$ are determined by a positive number $y>0$ and are of the form $T^*S^1/\Lambda_y \to S^1$ where $\Lambda_y$ is the period lattice determined by the integral affine structure corresponding to $y$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    Since $S^1$ has dimension one $H^2(\Lambda) = 0$ for any period lattice $\Lambda \subset T^*S^1$. One way to see this is by noting
    that $\pi_1(S^1) = \Z$ and the group cohomology of $\Z$ vanishes for $i>1$. Hence, every Lagrangian fibration
    admits a global section, and since the de Rham cohomology $H^2(S^1)=0$ there are no other considerations. Hence, the Lagrangian fibrations
    over $S^1$ are all topologically trivial, admit a global section and are of the form $T^*S^1/\Lambda_y \to S^1$ where $\Lambda_y$ is the lattice determined by $y>0$. They are classified by the integral affine structure on $S^1$ and by Lemma \ref{lemma:classification-integral-affine-circles} are therefore determined by
    a number $y>0$.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
    An integral affine structure on $S^1$ is equivalent to a full rank lattice in $T^*S^1 =  S^1 \times \R$ which is determined by the multiples of some number $y \in \R\setminus 0$. This
    determines the symplectic volume of the space $T^*B/\Lambda_y$. Different choices of $y$ change the symplectic volume of $T^*B/\Lambda_y$ and hence these bundles cannot be symplectomorphic for different choices of $y$.
\end{remark}

\section{Computing $H^2(\Lambda)$ for the torus}
We now use the group cohomology approach in order to calculate $H^2(\Lambda)$
for our compact integral affine surfaces. For $\mathbb{T}^2$, we have that
$\pi_1(\mathbb{T}^2) = \Z \oplus \Z$. The group ring for $G = \Z \oplus \Z$ is
given by $\Z[G] = \Z[x,x^{-1}, y, y^{-1}]$ where $x$ and $y$ are the generators
for $\Z \oplus \Z$.

We will make use of the Koszul resolution which provides us with a nice free
resolution of $\Z$ in this case.

\begin{definition}
    Let \(R^n\) be a free \(R\)-module with basis \(e_1,\dots,e_n\). The
    \(k\)-th exterior power \(\Lambda^k(R^n)\) is the free \(R\)-module generated by
    \[
        e_{i_1}\wedge \cdots \wedge e_{i_k},
        \qquad
        1 \le i_1 < \cdots < i_k \le n,
    \]
    where the wedge product is alternating:
    \[
        e_i \wedge e_j = -\, e_j \wedge e_i
    \]
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Koszul resolution \cite{Weibel_1994}]
    If $\textbf{x} = (x_1, \dots , x_n)$ is a regular sequence in \(R\), then the following sequence is exact:
    \[
        0 \longrightarrow \Lambda^n(R^n)
        \longrightarrow \cdots
        \longrightarrow \Lambda^2(R^n)
        \longrightarrow R^n
        \xrightarrow{x}
        R
        \longrightarrow R/I
        \longrightarrow 0.
    \]
\end{lemma}

We have that $(x-1, y-1)$ is a regular sequence in $\Z[G]$ as $x-1$ is a
non-zero divisor in $\Z[G]$ and $y-1$ is a non-zero divisor in $\Z[G]/(x-1) =
    \Z[y, y^{-1}]$. Since $Z[x,x^{-1}, y, y^{-1}]/\langle x-1, y-1 \rangle \cong
    \Z$, the Koszul complex yields the following exact sequence

$$
    0 \mapsto \Lambda^2(\Z[G]^2) \xrightarrow{d_2} \Z[G]^2 \xrightarrow{d_1} \Z[G] \to \Z \to 0
$$
where $\Lambda^2(\Z[G]^2) = \Z[G]$ and
\begin{align*}
    d_1(a,b) & = (x-1)a + (y-1)b,   \\
    d_2(c)   & = ((y-1)c , -(x-1)c)
\end{align*}

We apply $Hom_{\Z[G]}(-, \Z^2)$ to the deleted resolution which yields

$$
    0 \mapsto \Z^2 \xrightarrow{\tilde{d_1}} \Z^2 \oplus \Z^2 \xrightarrow{\tilde{d_2}} \Z^2 \to 0
$$

This immediately yields the following.

\begin{lemma}
    Let $G = \Z^2 = \langle x, y \rangle$ and let $\Z^2$ be a $G$-module. then the second group cohomology group is
    $$
        H^2(G, \Z^2) \cong \dfrac{\Z^2}{(x-1)\Z^2 +(y-1)\Z^2}
    $$
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    This follows from the above resolution.
    $$
        im(\tilde{d_2}) = -(x-1)\Z^2 +(y-1)\Z^2
    $$
    and the kernel of $\Z^2 \to 0$ is all of $\Z^2$.
\end{proof}

For the first family $\mathcal{A}_{a,b,c,d}$, the linear holonomy induces the
trivial representation of $G$ on $\Z^2$. We therefore have the following.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:chern-class-A}
    For $\mathcal{A}_{a,b,c,d}$, the cohomology group $H^2(G, \Z^2) = \Z^2$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    Both $(x-1)\Z=0$ and $(y-1)\Z=0$ as x and y act trivially. Therefore,
    \begin{align*}
        H^2(G, \Z^2) & = \dfrac{\Z^2}{(x-1)\Z^2 +(y-1)\Z^2} \\
                     & = \Z^2
    \end{align*}
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:chern-class-B}
    For $\mathcal{B}_{n,a,b}$, the cohomology group $H^2(G, \Z^2) = \dfrac{\Z}{n \Z} \oplus \Z$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    This amounts to computing the action of $(x-1)$ and $(y-1)$ on $\Z^2$ induced by the linear holonomy representation.
    From our classification in Theorem \ref{thm:classification-torus} we have that $x$ acts trivially and $y$ acts by
    $\begin{pmatrix}
            1 & n \\
            0 & 1
        \end{pmatrix}$. We calculate
    $$y\cdot (l,m) - (l,m) = (nl, 0)$$
    and so $(y-1)\Z^2 = n\Z \oplus 0$. This gives
    \begin{align*}
        H^2(G, \Z^2) & =\dfrac{\Z^2}{n\Z \oplus 0}  \\
                     & = \dfrac{\Z}{n \Z} \oplus \Z
    \end{align*}
\end{proof}

Since $H^3(\mathbb{T}^2, \R) = 0$, it follows from Proposition
\ref{prop:every:lagrangian-class} that every element in $H^2(\Lambda)$ is the
Chern class of some Lagrangian fibration. We summarize this data in the
following theorem.

\begin{restatable}{theorem}{chernTorus}
    The possible Chern classes for Lagrangian fibrations over the torus are:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item For the integral affine structures corresponding to $\mathcal{A}_{a,b,c,d}$,
              every element of the cohomology group $H^2(\Lambda) \cong \Z^2$ is the Chern
              class of some Lagrangian torus fibration.
        \item For the integral affine structures corresponding to $\mathcal{B}_{n,a,b}$,
              every element of the cohomology group $H^2(\Lambda) \cong \dfrac{\Z}{n \Z}
                  \oplus \Z$ is the Chern class of some Lagrangian torus fibration.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{restatable}

\section{Computing $H^2(\Lambda)$ for the Klein bottle}
\subsection{Spectral Sequences}\label{sec:spectral-seq}
The fundamental group of the Klein bottle is $\pi_1(K^2) = \Z \rtimes \Z$. In
this case, there is a nice spectral sequence that reduces computation of this
semi-direct product, to the computation of group cohomologies of $\Z$ with
respect to a representation on a certain first cohomology group. We will give a
quick outline of spectral sequences with a focus on how to use them to
calculate this group cohomology. We will closely follow \cite{McCleary2001}.

Consider generally, a cohomolgy theory and set $H^* = \bigoplus_i H^i$. The
goal of a spectral sequence is to approximate the graded object $H^*$ or in
some nice cases, to directly compute this. We make the assumption that $H^*$
has a filtration

$$
    H^*=F^0H^* \supseteq \dots \supseteq F^nH^* \supseteq F^{n+1}H^* \supseteq \dots \supseteq \{0\}
$$
and for now we will assume that $H^*$ is a graded vector space for simplicity. Given a filtration $F^*$ of $H^*$ it
can be collapsed into another graded vector space called \emph{the associated graded vector spcae} defined by
$$
    E_0^p(H^*) = F^pH^*/F^{p+1}H^*
$$

In the case that $H^*$ is a vector space we can recover $H^*$ up to isomorphism
by $$ H^* \cong \bigoplus_{p=0}^{\infty} E_0^p(H^*) = \bigoplus_{p=0}^{\infty}
    F^pH^*/F^{p+1}H^* $$ However if $H^*$ is not a graded vector space there can be
extension problems. We will not discuss this extension problem in more detail
as in our computations we will have only one graded piece to consider and hence
there is no extension problem.

Now, the idea is that $H^*$ may not be easily computed but we can try to
approximate it by the associated graded vector space to some filtration $F^*$
of $H^*$. We now observe that $E_0^*(H^*)$ is bigraded in the following sense.
If we define $F^pH^r = F^pH^* \cap H^r$ then we have that $$ E^{p,q}_0 =
    \dfrac{F^pH^{p+q}}{F^{p+1}H^{p+q}} $$ The index $q$ is called \emph{the
    complementary degree} and the index $p$ is called the \emph{filtration degree}.

We note that in the case of vector spaces we can recover $H^r$ as the direct
sum of the pieces of $E^{p,q}_0$ of total degree $r$ e.g. where $p+q=r$. We can
now give the definition of a spectral sequence.

\begin{definition}
    A first quadrant spectral sequence is a sequence of differential bigraded vector spaces. For every $r \in \mathbb{N}$ and $p,q>0$ we have
    a vector space $E^{p,q}_r$ equipped with a linear map
    $$
        d_r : E^{p,q}_r \to E^{p+r, q-r+1}
    $$
    with $d_r^2=0$ a differential of bidegree $(r, 1-r)$. Furthermore, for all $r geq 1$ we have that
    $E_{r+1}^{*,*} = H(E_{r}^{*,*}, d_r)$ where $H(E_{r}^{*,*}, d_r)$ denotes taking the cohomology at
    the $E_{r}^{*,*}$ spot.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    We call $E_r^{*,*}$ the $E_r$-page. Turning a page e.g. going from the $E_r$-page to the $E_{r+1}$-page corresponds to taking cohomology. The pages $E_r$ may
    be pictured as a lattice with each point a vector space and arrows between vector spaces corresponding to the differential $d_r$.
\end{remark}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/SpectralSequence.png}
    \caption{A page $E_r$ in a spectral sequence. Arrows mapping of the page correspond to zero mappings. \cite{McCleary2001}}
\end{figure}

We can now say what it means for a spectral sequence to converge to a graded
vector space.

\begin{definition}
    A spectral sequence $\{E_r^{*,*}, d_r\}$ of vector spaces is said to converge to a graded vector space $H^*$ if $H^*$ has a filtration
    $F^*$ and
    $$
        E_{\infty}^{p,q} \cong F^pH^{p+q}/F^{p+1}H^{p_q} = E_0^{p,q}(H^*)
    $$
\end{definition}

There are still some issues around uniqueness of the convergence that we shall
not discuss. We also formulated our definitions in terms of graded vector
spaces as oppose to more generally graded $R$-modules. The theory extends to
these in a very similar fashion except there are now more issues with
reconstructing our graded module $H^*$ as for example there may be multiple
extensions of a $R$-module $A$ by another $R$-module e.g. these are not all
split as in the vector space case.

We now give the spectral sequence that we shall use in order to compute the
group cohomology of $\pi_1(K) = \Z \rtimes \Z$.

\begin{lemma}[Hochschild-Lydon-Serre Spectral Sequence \cite{Hochschild1953CohomologyOG}]
    Let $G$ be a group and $N \subseteq G$ a normal subgroup and let $A$ be a $G$-module. Then there is a spectral sequence
    $$
        E_2^{p,q} = H^p(G/N, H^q(N, A)) \implies H^{p+q}(G, A)
    $$
\end{lemma}

For the fundamental group of the Klein bottle, we have the short exact sequence
$$ 0 \to \Z \to \Z \rtimes \Z \to \Z \to 0 $$

and so $\Z$ sits inside $\Z \rtimes \Z$ and $\dfrac{\Z \rtimes \Z}{\Z \times 0}
    \cong \Z$. The differential on the $E_2$ page is $$ d: E_2^{p,q} \to E_2^{p+2,
    q-1} $$ Using the fact that for any $\Z$-module $G$, the group cohomology
groups $H^i(\Z, G)=0$ for $i>1$ it follows that the only entry of the spectral
sequence we need to calculate is $H^1(\Z, H^1(\Z, A))$ where $A= \Z^2$ viewed
as a $\Z$-module.

\subsection{Calculating $H^1(\Z, H^1(\Z, A))$ }

Let $G=\Z\langle a \rangle$ and let $A=\Z^2$ be a $G$-module. A free resolution
of $\Z[G]$ is given by $$ 0 \to \Z[G] \xrightarrow{a-1} \Z[G]
    \xrightarrow{\epsilon} \Z \to 0 $$ where $\epsilon$ is the augmentation map.
Applying $\text{Hom}_{\Z[G]}(-, A)$ to the deleted resolution yields

$$
    0 \to A \to A \to 0
$$

and so the cohomology group is given by

$$
    H^1(G, A) = \dfrac{A}{(a-1)A}
$$

Now in order to calculate the full cohomology group $H^1(\Z, H^1(\Z, A))$,
there is the question of what the $\Z$-module structure on $H^1(\Z, A)$ should
be.

\begin{lemma}
    The action of $Z\langle b \rangle$ on $H^1(G, A)$ is given by
    $$
        ( b \cdot c)(a) = -(ba^{-1})c(a)
    $$
    where $c \in \text{Der}(G, A)$ and $G = \langle a \rangle$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    We have that $H^1(G, A) = \dfrac{\text{Der}(G, A)}{\text{PDer}(G, A)}$. Let $c \in \text{Der}(G, A)$. Then
    $$
        (b\cdot c)(a_1) = b\cdot c(b^{-1}a_1 b)
    $$
    where this action is as in \cite{Hochschild1953CohomologyOG}. Now, using that $c$ is a derivation
    \begin{align*}
        c(a^{-1}a) & = c(1)                   \\
                   & = 0                      \\
                   & = c(a^{-1}) + a^{-1}c(a)
    \end{align*}
    and so  $c(a^{-1}) = -a^{-1}c(a)$. This gives us that
    \begin{align*}
        b\cdot c(b^{-1}a b) & = b \cdot c(a^{-1})    \\
                            & = (-ba^{-1})\cdot c(a)
    \end{align*}
    as required.
\end{proof}

This gives the following expression for the relevant cohomology group keeping
in mind the respective actions of $\Z\langle a \rangle$ and $\Z\langle b
    \rangle$.
\begin{corollary}
    The cohomology group $H^1(\Z\langle b \rangle, H^1(\Z\langle a \rangle, A))$ can be expressed as
    $$
        \dfrac{\dfrac{A}{(a-1)A}}{(b-1) \dfrac{A}{(a-1)A}}
    $$
\end{corollary}

Now it remains to calculate this group for the holonomy representations of the
integral affine structures on the Klein bottle. Firstly, recall that the
integral affine structures on the Klein bottle are given by the following.

The possible integral affine structures on the Klein bottle are
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $a = \left(I, \begin{pmatrix}
                  0 \\ x_2
              \end{pmatrix}\right), \quad b = \left(\begin{pmatrix}
                  1 & 0  \\
                  0 & -1
              \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}
                  y_1 \\ 0
              \end{pmatrix}\right)$, with $x_2, y_1>0$.
    \item $a = \left(\begin{pmatrix}
                  1 & n \\
                  0 & 1
              \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}
                  0 \\ x_2
              \end{pmatrix}\right), \quad b = \left(\begin{pmatrix}
                  1 & 0  \\
                  0 & -1
              \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}
                  y_1 \\ x_2
              \end{pmatrix}\right)$, with $x_2, y_1>0$.
    \item $a = \left(I, \begin{pmatrix}
                  x_1 \\ -x_1
              \end{pmatrix}\right), \quad b = \left(\begin{pmatrix}
                  0 & 1 \\
                  1 & 0
              \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}
                  y_1 \\ 0
              \end{pmatrix}\right)$, with $x_1, y_1>0$.
    \item $a = \left(\begin{pmatrix}
                      1 & n \\
                      0 & 1
                  \end{pmatrix} , \begin{pmatrix}
                      0 \\ x_2
                  \end{pmatrix}\right), \quad b =\left(\begin{pmatrix}
                      1 & 1  \\
                      0 & -1
                  \end{pmatrix} , \begin{pmatrix}
                      y_1 \\ \frac{n-1}{n}x_2
                  \end{pmatrix}\right)$, with $y_1> 0$ and \\
          $2y_1 \neq -\frac{n-1}{n}x_2$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{restatable}{theorem}{kleinChern} \label{thm:Klein-chern}
    The possible Chern classes for Lagrangian fibrations over the Klein bottle are
    $$H^2(\Lambda) \cong \begin{cases}
            \Z/ 2\Z \oplus \Z, \quad \text{for family 1).}                                  \\
            \Z/ 2\Z \oplus \Z, \quad \text{for family 2) with } n \equiv 0 \text{ mod }  2. \\
            \Z, \quad \text{ otherwise}.
        \end{cases}$$
\end{restatable}

\begin{proof}
    \begin{case}[Family 1]
        In this case the action of $\Z\langle a \rangle$ on $A = \Z^2$ is trivial and hence $(a-1)A = 0$. The action of
        $b$ on $\dfrac{A}{(a-1)A}$ is given by the matrix action of $-(ba^{-1})$. Since $a$ acts as the identity the action is given by

        \begin{align*}
            b \cdot \begin{pmatrix}
                        x_1 \\
                        y_1
                    \end{pmatrix} & = \begin{pmatrix}
                                          -1 & 0 \\
                                          0  & 1
                                      \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}
                                                       x_1 \\
                                                       y_1
                                                   \end{pmatrix} \\
                                    & =   \begin{pmatrix}
                                              -x_1 \\
                                              y_1
                                          \end{pmatrix}
        \end{align*}
        and hence the action by $(b-1)$ is
        \begin{align*}
            (b-1) \cdot \begin{pmatrix}
                            x_1 \\
                            y_1
                        \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
                                            -2x_1 \\
                                            0
                                        \end{pmatrix}
        \end{align*}
        This gives us that $(b-1)\cdot \dfrac{A}{(a-1)A} = 2\Z \oplus 0$. Taking the quotient of $A = \Z^2$ by this gives the required result.
    \end{case}

    \begin{case}[Family 2]
        In this case, $a$ acts non-trivially. The action of $(a-1)$ is given by
        $$
            (a-1)\begin{pmatrix}
                x \\
                y
            \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
                ny \\
                0
            \end{pmatrix}
        $$
        It follows that
        $$
            \dfrac{A}{(a-1)A} = \dfrac{\Z}{n\Z} \oplus \Z
        $$
        We calculate $-ba^{-1}$ to be $\begin{pmatrix}
                -1 & n \\
                0  & 1
            \end{pmatrix}$.
    \end{case}
    and so
    \begin{align*}
        \begin{pmatrix}
            -1 & n \\
            0  & 1
        \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}
                          x_1 \\
                          y_1
                      \end{pmatrix} & = \begin{pmatrix}
                                            ny_1 - x_1 \\
                                            y_1
                                        \end{pmatrix}  \\
                                      & =\begin{pmatrix}
                                             - x_1 \\
                                             y_1
                                         \end{pmatrix}
    \end{align*}
    where in the last equality we are reducing modulo n in the first component. Therefore the action of $(b-1)$ on
    $\begin{pmatrix}
            x_1 \\
            y_1
        \end{pmatrix} \in \dfrac{A}{(a-1)A}$ is given by
    $$
        (b-1)\cdot \begin{pmatrix}
            x_1 \\
            y_1
        \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
            -2x_1 \\
            0
        \end{pmatrix}
    $$
    and so

    \begin{align*}
        H^2(\Lambda) = H^1(\Z, H^1(\Z, A)) & = \dfrac{\dfrac{\Z}{n\Z} \oplus \Z}{\langle \tilde{2} \rangle \oplus 0} \\
                                           & = \dfrac{\Z}{\text{gcd}(2,n) \Z}\oplus \Z
    \end{align*}
    giving the required result for the second family.

    \begin{case}[Family 3]
        In this case, $a$ again acts trivially and so it remains to calculate the action of $b$. We compute
        \begin{align*}
            -ba^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}
                           0  & -1 \\
                           -1 & 0
                       \end{pmatrix}
        \end{align*}
        and so $(b-1)\cdot \begin{pmatrix}
                x \\ y
            \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
                -y-x \\ -y-x
            \end{pmatrix}$
        and so it follows that
        \begin{align*}
            H^1(\Z, H^1(\Z, A)) & = \dfrac{\Z \oplus \Z}{\langle (1,1) \rangle} \\
                                & = \Z
        \end{align*}
    \end{case}

    \begin{case}[Family 4]
        In this case, the $\Z\langle a\rangle$ action on $\Z$ is the same as in Family 2. It remains to calculate the action of $b$. We calculate
        $$
            -(ba^{-1}) = \begin{pmatrix}
                -1 & n-1 \\
                0  & 1
            \end{pmatrix}
        $$
        and so $(b-1)\cdot \begin{pmatrix}
                x \\
                y
            \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
                -2x +ny - y \\
                0
            \end{pmatrix}$. Since this action is on $\dfrac{\Z}{n \Z} \oplus \Z$ we reduce mod $n$ in the first component. This now gives that

        \begin{align*}
            H^1(\Z, H^1(\Z, A)) & = \dfrac{\dfrac{\Z}{n\Z} \oplus \Z}{\dfrac{\Z}{n\Z} \oplus 0} \\
                                & = \Z
        \end{align*}
        and this completes the calculations. \qedhere
    \end{case}
\end{proof}

\section{Constructing the fibrations from their invariants.}\label{sec:construct}
We now show how to construct the Lagrangian fibrations over the torus and the
Klein bottle from their invariants. Let $B$ denote either the torus or the
Klein bottle.

From taking the long exact sequence in cohomology associated to the short exact
sequence of sheafs $$ 0 \to \Lambda \to T^*B \to T^*B/ \Lambda \to 0 $$ and
using that $T^*B$ is a fine sheaf we obtain that $H^1(T^*B\Lambda) \cong
    H^2(\Lambda)$. We will make use of the following interpretation of
$H^1(T^*B/\Lambda)$.

We can view the torus or the Klein bottle as a square with the usual
identifications which we will denote by $\Sigma$ in both cases. Take a small
disc $D$ in the interior of this square such that the bundle is trivial over
this disc. Then a section of this bundle on the boundary of this disc is just a
map from $S^1 \to T^2$ and these are determined up to homotopy by $\pi_1(T^2)$.
A section on $\partial D$ can be extended onto all of $D$ if and only if it
corresponds to the zero homotopy class.

Now consider the sections on $\partial D$ which are extendable on $\Sigma
    \setminus D$. This forms some subgroup $F \subset \pi_1(T^2)$. We now have the
following lemma.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:quotient-chern-class}
    The group $H^1(T^*B/\Lambda)$ is isomorphic to $\dfrac{\pi_1(T^2)}{F}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}

    We can cover the Klein bottle or the torus, which we have denoted both by
    $\Sigma$, by two open sets $U$ and $V$. Let $U$ be a neighborhood of the disc
    $D$ and let $V$ be a neighborhood of $\Sigma \setminus D$. We consider the
    Mayer-Vietoris sequence for the sheaf $T^*B/\Lambda$.
    \begin{align*}
        \dots \to H^0(U) \oplus H^0(V) \xrightarrow{d} H^0(U \cap V) \to H^1(\Sigma) \to H^1(U) \oplus H^1(V) \to \dots .
    \end{align*}
    Now we claim that $H^1(U) \oplus H^1(V) = 0$. Indeed, they both sit into the short exact sequence
    $$
        0 \to \Lambda|_V \to T^*B|_V \to T^*B/\Lambda|_V \to 0
    $$

    and we note that restriction preserves exactness as exactness can be checked
    stalk-wise. Taking the corresponding long exact sequence in cohomology and
    using the fact that $T^*B|_V$ is a fine sheaf, we conclude that $H^1(T^*B|_V)
        \cong H^2(\Lambda|_V)$. Now, by Theorem \ref{thm:equiv-cohomology-theories} the
    cohomology $H^2(\Lambda|_V)$ can be calculated using singular cohomological
    methods and even via cellular methods. Theorem \ref{thm:homo-inv} gives us
    homotopy invariance of this group and since $V$ deformation retracts onto the
    wedge of two circles this cohomology group vanishes as there are no two cells.

    We can apply the exact same arguments to $U$ except now $U$ is contractible and
    so $H^2(\Lambda|_U)=0$. It follows that $H^1(U) \oplus H^1(V) = 0$.

    We therefore have from the Mayer-Vietoris sequence that $H^1(\Sigma) \cong
        \dfrac{H^0(U \cap V)}{\Im d}$. We now show that $\dfrac{H^0(U \cap V)}{\Im d}
        \cong \dfrac{\pi_1(T^2)}{F}$.

    Every section of $H^0(U \cap V)$ corresponds to a section on $\partial D =
        S^1$. We consider the map $$ \varphi: H^0(U \cap V) \to \dfrac{\pi_1(T^2)}{F}
    $$ $$ s \mapsto [s] $$ which maps a section to its corresponding homotopy
    class. It is clear that this map is surjective.

    Its kernel consists of the maps which are extendable on all of $\Sigma
        \setminus D$. Recall that the map $d$ is given by $$ d([u] + [v]) = [u|_{U \cap
        V}]- [v|_{U \cap V}] $$ where $[u] + [v] \in H^0(U) \oplus H^0(V)$. But a
    section over $U$ corresponded to the zero homotopy class and hence vanishes
    under $\varphi$. Similarly, $[v|_{U \cap V}]$ came from a section on $\Sigma
        \setminus D$ and hence $\varphi([v|_{U \cap V}]) = 0$. It follows that $\Im d
        \subseteq \ker \varphi$.

    Conversely, suppose that $\varphi([s]) = 0$. Then this implies that $[s]$ is
    extendable on $\Sigma\setminus D$. But this is exactly the elements of $H^0(V)$
    and hence we have $\ker \varphi \subseteq \Im d$ and the result follows.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The construction}
We fix the integral affine structure on the base of our compact surface $B$. In
order to construct a Lagrangian fibration over the base with a given Chern
class we first start with the symplectic reference bundle $T^*B/\Lambda$. Now,
we choose one of the fibres of the fibration - i.e. a Lagrangian torus in the
total space of our bundle - and perform Luttinger surgery on this torus with
respect to some section $s$ over the boundary of a small disc.

If this disc corresponds to the homotopy class $(m,n) \in \pi_1(T^2)$, then the
Luttinger surgery maps this section to the zero section and hence induces
addition by $-(m,n)$ in $\pi_1(T^2)$. We have from Lemma
\ref{lemma:quotient-chern-class} that the group $H^2(\Lambda)$ which
corresponds to the Chern classes is a quotient of $\pi_1(T^2)$. It follows that
we can produce any Chern class by picking our section $s$ to lie in the
appropriate homotopy class.

In the case of the Klein bottle, we are now done as there are no symplectic
considerations. In the case of the torus, we must construct the symplectically
different fibrations with the same Chern class.

By Remark \ref{prop:classifying-group} and Proposition
\ref{prop:generated-shifts} the Lagrangian classes mapping to a fixed Chern
class is a quotient $\dfrac{H^2(B, \R)}{S}$ where $S$ is the subgroup generated
by cohomological invariants of a shift. Since our Lagrangian fibration can
always be reduced to one with a global section by means of Luttinger surgery it
follows that we can construct the symplectic type by adding an element $[h] \in
    H^2(B, \R)$ considered up to cohomological invariants of shifts. These
invariants are calculated in Theorem \ref{thm:invar-calculation} and this
completes the construction.
