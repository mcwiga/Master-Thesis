\chapter{Lagrangian Fibrations and Integral Affine Manifolds}\label{LagrangianFibrations}
\section{Symplectic Geometry}

In this section we will recall some of the basics of symplectic geometry and
Lagrangian submanifolds.

Symplectic geometry has its roots in classical mechanics as the phase space of
a dynamical system naturally has the structure of a symplectic manifold. In
symplectic geometry it is often global properties that are of interest. In
fact, it turns out that all symplectic manifolds are locally equivalent in
contrast to Riemannian geometry where curvature plays the role of a local
invariant. In this chapter we will give an extremely brief introduction to
symplectic geometry. We will then describe the relationship between integral
affine structures and symplectic geometry. Indeed, in Chapter \ref{ch:prelim}
we have seen that an integral affine structure is equivalent to a lattice in
the cotangent bundle. It will turn out that the cotangent bundle of any
manifold inherits the structure of a symplectic manifold and this will serve as
the starting point for out discussion.

We start with some of the basic definitions and properties of symplectic
manifolds.

\begin{definition}[Symplectic Manifold]
    A symplectic manifold $(M, \omega)$ consists of a smooth manifold $M$ and a closed 2-form $\omega$ such that $\omega$ is non-degenerate meaning the bi-linear form $\omega_p$ is non-degenerate on $T_pM$ for all $p \in M$.
\end{definition}

Non-degeneracy of the symplectic form gives a canonical way to identify the
tangent and cotangent bundle of a symplectic manifold. This is done via the map
$$ \tilde{\omega}|_p : T_pM \to T_p^*M $$ $$ v \mapsto \omega_p(v, \cdot)$$ for
$v \in T_pM$. This is an isomorphism by non-degeneracy of the symplectic form.
This identification leads to the notion of a Hamiltonian vector field.

\begin{definition}[Hamiltonian Vector Field]
    To any $H\in C^{\infty}(M)$ we can associate a unique Hamiltonian vector field $X_H$ by
    $$\iota_{X_H}\omega := \omega(X_H, \cdot) = dH$$
\end{definition}

For mathematicians there is generally no distinguished choice of Hamiltonian
function $H$. However, physicists often pick a Hamiltonian function based on
physical criteria and are generally interested in studying the flow of this
Hamiltonian vector field which, for instance, recovers Hamilton's equations.

\subsection{The Cotangent Bundle}
One of the most important examples of symplectic manifolds is the cotangent
bundle, which naturally inherits the structure of a symplectic manifold. In
fact, Darboux's theorem states that every symplectic manifold looks locally
like a cotangent bundle. This class of symplectic manifolds will play a crucial
role throughout this thesis and we will see now exactly how the cotangent
bundle obtains a symplectic structure.

\begin{eg}[Cotangent Bundle]
    Let $Q$ be any smooth manifold and $M = T^*Q$ its cotangent bundle.
    We will now illustrate that there is a canonical one-form $\theta \in \Omega^1(T^*Q)$
    and show that the exterior derivative $-d\theta$ defines a symplectic structure on $T^*Q$.

    Let $\pi : T^*Q \to Q$ be the canonical projection map. Then for $X_p \in
        T_p(T^*Q)$ we define $\langle \theta_p , X_p \rangle := \langle p , d_p\pi(X_p)
        \rangle$ where $\langle \cdot , \cdot \rangle$ denotes contraction of a vector
    and covector. This canonical one-form satisfies the following important
    property.
    \begin{lemma}
        The canonical one-form $\theta$ is the unique one-form such that for any other one-form $\alpha \in \Omega^1(Q)$
        $$\alpha = \alpha^* \theta$$
        where we view $\alpha$ as a section $\alpha: Q \to T^*Q$.
    \end{lemma}
    \begin{proof}
        Let $w \in T_qQ$. Then we have that
        \begin{align*}
            \langle (\alpha^*\theta)_q , w \rangle & = \langle \theta_{\alpha_q} , d_q\alpha (w) \rangle \\
                                                   & = \langle \alpha_q , w \rangle
        \end{align*}
        Uniqueness is obtained by noting that every tangent vector $v \in T_p(T^*Q)$ can be written in the form $d_q\alpha(w)$
        except for those in the kernel of $d_p\pi$. But the vectors not in this kernel form an open, dense set and so by continuity we get uniqueness.
    \end{proof}

    We can also find an expression for this canonical one-form in coordinates.

    Let $(U, q_1, \dots, q_n)$ be a coordinate chart on $Q$. We denote the vector
    fields corresponding to these coordinates by
    \begin{align*}
        \dfrac{\partial}{\partial q_1}, \dots ,  \dfrac{\partial}{\partial q_n} \in \mathfrak{X}(U).
    \end{align*}
    These vector fields define functions on each fibre of $T^*Q|_U$ by
    \begin{align*}
        p_i : T^*_xQ|_u \to \R \\
        \eta \mapsto \langle \eta, \dfrac{\partial}{\partial q_i}|_x \rangle
    \end{align*}
    where $\langle \cdot , \cdot \rangle$ is the canonical pairing between vectors and covectors. The  set of functions
    \begin{align*}
        \pi^*q_1, \dots \pi^* q_n, p_1, \dots , p_n \in C^{\infty}(T^*Q|_U)
    \end{align*}
    is a set of coordinates on $T^*Q|_U$. We will often abuse notation and write the $\pi^* q_i$ as simply $q_i$.
    \begin{lemma}
        In local cotangent coordinates $q_1, \dots , q_n, p_1, \dots , p_n$ on $T^*Q$, the canonical one-form is given by
        $$\theta|_{T^*U} = \sum_j p_j dq_j.$$
    \end{lemma}
    \begin{proof}
        We let $\alpha = \sum_j \alpha_j dq_j$ be a $1$-form on $U$. We have that $\alpha^*p_j = \alpha_j$ and $\alpha^*q_j = q_j$ which gives that
        \begin{align*}
            \alpha^*\theta|_{T^*U} & = \alpha^* \sum_j p_j dq_j \\
                                   & =\sum_j \alpha_j dq_j      \\
                                   & = \alpha.\qedhere
        \end{align*}
    \end{proof}

    Putting this together we obtain a symplectic form on $T^*Q$.
    \begin{theorem}
        The $2$-form $-d\theta$ defines a symplectic form on $T^*Q$.
    \end{theorem}
    \begin{proof}
        In local coordinates we have that $\omega = \sum_j dq_j \wedge dp_j$ which is clearly closed and non-degenerate.
    \end{proof}
\end{eg}

We will also wish to speak about maps between the symplectic manifolds. The
correct notion of "isomorphism" in this category is the following.

\begin{definition}
    Let $(M, \omega)$ and $(N, \omega')$ be two symplectic manifolds. Then a symplectomorphism between $M$ and $N$ is a diffeomorphism
    $$\varphi: M \to N$$
    such that $\omega = \varphi^* \omega'$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Lagrangian Submanifolds}
The goal of this thesis is to classify Lagrangian fibrations over compact
surfaces. In this section we will introduce the concept of Lagrangian
submanifolds and prove some basic results regarding these.
\begin{definition}[Symplectic Orthogonal]
    Let $(M, \omega)$ be a symplectic manifold and let $N \subset M$ be a submanifold. The symplectic orthogonal at a point $p \in M$ is
    $$(T_pN)^{\omega} := \{v \in T_pM \mid \omega(v, w) = 0, \forall w \in N\}$$
\end{definition}

\begin{itemize}
    \item If $(T_pN)^{\omega} \subset T_pN$ then $T_pN$ is said to be \emph{coisotropic}.
    \item If $T_pN\subset (T_pN)^{\omega} $ then $T_pN$ is said to be \emph{isotropic}.
    \item If $(T_pN)^{\omega} = T_pN$ then $T_pN$ is said to be \emph{Lagrangian}.
    \item If $(T_pN)^{\omega} \cap T_pN = \empty$ then $T_pN$ is said to be
          \emph{symplectic}.
\end{itemize}

A submanifold $N$ of a symplectic manifold $M$ is said to be coisotropic
(isotropic, Lagrangian, symplectic) if its tangent space is a coisotropic
(isotropic, Lagrangian, symplectic) subspace of $T_pM$ at every point $p \in
    M$.

\begin{remark}
    We note that a submanifold $\iota : N \to M$ is isotropic if $\iota^*\omega =0$ and furthermore, it is Lagrangian if we also have
    $\text{dim }N = \frac{1}{2}\text{dim }M$.
\end{remark}

We will make use of the following lemma.
\begin{lemma}
    \label{lemma:cotangent-lagrangian-sub}
    The graph $\Gamma_{\alpha} \subset T^*Q$ of a one-form $\alpha$ is Lagrangian if and only if $\alpha$ is closed.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    It is clear that $\text{dim }\Gamma_{\alpha} =\frac{1}{2}\text{dim }T^*Q$. We compute
    \begin{align*}
        \alpha^*\omega & = -\alpha^*d\theta   \\
                       & = - d\alpha^* \theta \\
                       & = - d\alpha
    \end{align*}
    and so $\alpha^* \omega = 0 $ if and only if $\alpha$ is closed completing the proof by the previous remark.
\end{proof}

The cotangent bundle $T^*Q$ also gives rise to another Lagrangian submanifold.

\begin{eg}
    Let $\pi : T^*Q \to Q$ and fix $x \in Q$. Then $\pi^{-1}(x) = T_x^*Q$ is a Lagrangian submanifold inside $(T^*Q, \omega)$. It is easy to see that $\text{dim }T_x^*Q = \frac{1}{2}\text{dim }T^*Q $.
    Letting $i: T_x^*Q \hookrightarrow T^*Q$ be the inclusion and considering local coordinates $(q_1, \dots , q_n, p_1 \dots p_n)$ around $x$ and writing $\omega = \sum_j dq_j \wedge dp_j$ it follows that $\omega|_{T_x^*Q} = 0$ as $i^*(\sum_j dq_j \wedge dp_j) = \sum_j d(i^*q_j) \wedge d(i^*p_j)$ and
    $d(i^*q_i) = 0$ as $i^*q_i$ is constant.
\end{eg}

\section{Lagrangian Fibrations}

One of the goals of this thesis is to classify the Lagrangian fibrations over
closed surfaces. In this section we will give an overview of these objects and
give some examples and basic results surrounding them. We will then describe
how the base space of a Lagrangian fibration inherits an integral affine
structure.

\subsection{Definition of Lagrangian Fibration}
\begin{definition}
    A Lagrangian fibration $\pi: (M, \omega) \to B $ is a surjective map whose regular fibres are Lagrangian
    submanifolds of $(M, \omega)$.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}
    A Lagrangian fibration is called \emph{regular} if every fibre is regular. This means that for every point $q \in \pi^{-1}(p)$, the map $d\pi_q$ is surjective where $p \in B$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}

    For most of this thesis we will assume that $\pi$ is a surjective submersion
    and a proper map so that by a theorem of Ehresmann in \cite{Ehresmann1951}, the
    fibration $\pi : (M, \omega) \to B$ is a fiber bundle and the fibres are
    compact. We will sometimes refer to these as \emph{Lagrangian torus fibrations}
    as in this case the fibres turn out to be tori as we shall soon see.
\end{remark}

\begin{eg}
    We saw in Lemma \ref{lemma:cotangent-lagrangian-sub} that the fibres of $\pi: (T^*Q, \omega_{can}) \to Q$ are Lagrangian submanifolds
    and hence this is a Lagrangian fibration.
\end{eg}

\begin{eg}
    Let $Q= (\R / \Z)^n = \mathbb{T}^n$ be the $n$-torus. Then we have that $T^*(\mathbb{T}^n) = \mathbb{T}^n \times \R^n$ and the map
    $$
        \pi : T^*(\mathbb{T}^n) \to \R^n
    $$
    is a Lagrangian fibration with fibre $\mathbb{T}^n$.
\end{eg}
\begin{definition}[Shift by a one-form.] \label{def:shift-by-form}
    Let $\pi : (T^*Q, \omega_{can}) \to Q$ and fix $\alpha \in \Omega^1(Q)$. We define the \emph{shift by $\alpha$} to be the map
    $$ s_{\alpha}: T^*Q \to T^*Q$$
    $$ \beta_q \mapsto \beta_q + \alpha_q.$$
\end{definition}
\begin{prop} \label{prop:shift-by-closed}
    This defines a symplectomorphism of $T^*Q$ if and only if $d\alpha = 0$.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
    This map is a fibrewise translation and hence is obviously a diffeomorphism. We now check when it preserves
    the symplectic form. We calculate
    \begin{align*}
        s_{\alpha}^*\omega_{can} & = -s_{\alpha}^*d\theta   \\
                                 & = -ds_{\alpha}^* \theta. \\
    \end{align*}
    Thus it remains to see how $s_{\alpha}$ acts on the canonical one-form. We compute
    \begin{align*}
        s_{\alpha}^* \theta (v)|_{\beta_q} & = \theta_{\beta_q + \alpha_q}(ds_{\alpha}(v))     \\
                                           & = (\beta_q +\alpha_q)(d(\pi \circ s_{\alpha})(v)) \\
                                           & = (\beta_q +\alpha_q)(v)                          \\
                                           & = \theta_{\beta_q}(v) + \alpha_q(v)
    \end{align*}
    and so $s_{\alpha}^* \theta = \theta + \alpha$. Now
    \begin{align*}
        -ds_{\alpha}^* \theta & = -d\theta - d\alpha     \\
                              & = \omega_{can} - d\alpha
    \end{align*}
    and hence this defines a symplectomorphism if and only if $d\alpha = 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    We now note that given any Lagrangian section of $T^*Q$, i.e. a section determined by a closed one-form, we can shift this section to the zero section
    since shifts by closed one-forms are symplectomorphisms. This implies that if we consider $T^*Q$ as an abstract Lagrangian bundle it has no marked zero section and
    so the fibres do not have a canonical vector space structure, only the structure of an affine space.
\end{remark}

We now give the notion of equivalence of Lagrangian bundles.

\begin{definition}
    Let $M \to B$ and $N \to B$ be two Lagrangian fibrations over $B$. Then a \emph{Lagrangian equivalence} between
    $M \to B$ and $N \to B$ is given by
    \begin{center}
        \begin{tikzcd}
            M \arrow[r,"\Phi"] \arrow[dr] & N \arrow[d] \\
            & B
        \end{tikzcd}
    \end{center}
    where $\Phi$ is a fibrewise symplectomorphism i.e. a symplectomorphism that is also a bundle map.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
    There is no loss in generality in assuming that $\Phi$ covers the identity on $B$.  Indeed if $f: B \to B$
    is a diffeomorphism then there exists a fibrewise symplectomorphism $M$ and $N$ covering $f$ if and only if
    there exists a fibrewise symplectomorphsim between $M$ and the pullback bundle $f^*N$ covering the identity on $B$.
\end{remark}
\section{Integral Affine Structure on the Base}
We now discuss how the base space of a Lagrangian fibration inherits an
integral affine structure. This structure will be an invariant of the
fibration.

\subsection{Torus Bundles}
Throughout this section let $Q$ be a manifold and let $G$ be a Lie group.
\begin{definition}
    Suppose that $\dim Q = \dim G$. We say that $Q$ is a \emph{principal
        homogeneous G-space} if there is a free, transitive action of $G$ on $Q$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
    For any $q \in Q$ we have that $Q = G\cdot q \cong G$ as the action is transitive. For any $q' \in Q$ there exists a $g \in Q$
    such that $g \cdot q = q'$. Therefore, any two such identifications differ by an element of $G$.
\end{remark}
\begin{definition}
    If $G \cong (\R / \Z)^n$ and $Q$ is a principal
    homogeneous $G$-space, we call $Q$ an \emph{affine torus}. If $G$ is a vector space we call $Q$ an \emph{affine vector space}.
\end{definition}

Now we consider the case that $G:=V$ is a vector space. Suppose now that the
action is transitive but \emph{not necessarily free}. We define $$V_q := \{v
    \in V \mid v \cdot q = q\}$$.
\begin{prop}
    $V_q$ is a discrete subgroup of $V$ independent of $q \in Q$.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
    As a topological space, $Q = V/V_q$. Consider the map
    $$
        \phi_q : V \to Q
    $$
    $$ v \mapsto v \cdot q$$
    Taking the differential $(d\phi_q)_0 : T_0 V \to T_0Q$ and applying the rank-nullity theorem
    we find that $\dim V_q = 0$ and hence it is a discrete subgroup. We now show it is independent of $q$.

    For any $q' \in Q$, by transitivity of the action, there exists $w \in V$ such
    that $w \cdot q = q'$ and so for $v \in V_q$, $w \in V_{q'}$
    \begin{align*}
        (w^{-1}vw)\cdot q           & = q        \\
        \implies (w^{-1}v) \cdot q' & = q        \\
        \implies   v \cdot q'       & = w\cdot q \\
        \implies v \cdot q'         & = q
    \end{align*}
    and hence $V_q = V_{q'}$
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}\label{rem:torus-structure}
    This implies that $V_q$ is a lattice, i.e. $V_q \cong \Z^n$. We therefore have that $V/V_q \cong \R^n / \Z^k \cong \R^{n-k} \times \mathbb{T}^k$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
    It follows from our previous definitions that $Q$ is the product of an affine torus and an affine vector space.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Group Bundle]
    A group bundle $\pi : \mathcal{G} \to B$ is a fibre bundle such that each fibre is equipped with a group structure and there are charts
    $\pi^{-1}(U) \cong U \times G$ that are fibrewise isomorphisms with a fixed group $G$.
\end{definition}
We can define the notion of an action of a group bundle on any fibre bundle over the same base.

\begin{definition}
    Let $\mathcal{G} \to B$ be a group bundle and $M \to B$ be a fibre bundle. An action of $\mathcal{G} \to B$ on $M \to B$ is a smooth map
    $$\varphi : \mathcal{G} \times_B M \to M$$
    that is a fibrewise group action.
\end{definition}

\begin{eg}
    Let $\mathcal{T} \to B$ be a torus bundle. Then an affine torus bundle $M \to B$ is a fiber bundle
    with a free, transitive action of $\mathcal{T} \to B$. We therefore have by Remark \ref{rem:torus-structure} that if $M \to B$ is a fiber bundle with compact
    fibres and $E \to B$ is a vector bundle with a transitive action on $M \to B$ such that $\dim M = \dim E$, then $M$ inherits the structure of an affine torus
    bundle.
\end{eg}

Suppose that we have an action as in the above example. Then if we let \[\Lambda
    = \bigsqcup_p \Lambda_p\]
be the bundle of stabilisers of the action of $E \to B$ on $M \to B$, i.e.
$\Lambda_p\subset E_p$ is the isotropy group at $p \in B$, then we can form a
torus bundle $$ \mathcal{T} := E/\Lambda \to B. $$ In this way $M \to B$
becomes an affine torus bundle since $\mathcal{T}$ now acts freely on $M$ as we
have quotiented out the fixed points.

Suppose now that we have a global section $\sigma: B \to M$. Then $M \to B$
becomes a torus bundle as we can use the global section to pick out an identity
- see Lemma \ref{lemma:identification-of-bundle}.

\begin{theorem} \label{thm:cotangent-action}
    Let $(M, \omega)$ be a symplectic manifold and let $\pi: M \to B$ be a Lagrangian fibration with compact connected fibres.
    Then, there is a canonical, fibrewise transitive action of the vector bundle $T^*B \to B$ on $M \to B$. Therefore, $M \to B$ can be canonically equipped with
    the structure of an affine torus bundle.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Let $VM \subset TM$ be the vertical bundle i.e. $V_pM = \ker d_p\pi$. Since $\pi: M \to B$ is a Lagrangian fibration it follows that $VM \subset TM$ is a Lagrangian subbundle as
    it is tangent to the fibres of the fibration. Let $\alpha \in \Omega^1(B)$ be any $1$-form and let $X_{\alpha} \in \mathfrak{X}$ be the vector field defined by
    $$
        \iota_{X_{\alpha}}\omega = -\pi^*\alpha.
    $$
    Let $Y \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$ be a vertical vector field i.e. $d\pi(Y(p)) = 0$ for all $p \in M$. Then we have
    \begin{align*}
        \omega(X_{\alpha}, Y) & = \iota_Y \iota_{X_{\alpha}} \omega \\
                              & = -\iota_Y\pi^*\alpha               \\
                              & = 0
    \end{align*}
    as $Y$ is vertical. Now as $VM^{\omega} = VM$, this implies that $X_{\alpha}$ is vertical. We note that the value of $X_{\alpha}$ at $m \in M$ depends only on the value of $\alpha_{\pi(m)}$ as it is defined through pulling back $\alpha$.
    We have thus assigned to each $\alpha_{\pi(m)}$ with $m \in M$, a vector in the vertical bundle $VM$ by $X_{\alpha} (m)$ depending only on the value of $\alpha_{\pi(m)}$. Moreover, this is assignment is linear since
    \begin{align*}
        \iota_{X_{a\alpha + b\beta}}\omega & = -\pi^* (a\alpha + b\beta)       \\
                                           & = -a\pi^* (\alpha) -b\pi^*(\beta)
    \end{align*}
    for $a,b \in \R$ and $\alpha, \beta \in \Omega^1(B)$. We thus have a  isomorphism
    $$
        V_m M \cong T^*_{\pi(m)}B
    $$
    and putting together all these isomorphisms yields a bundle isomorphism
    $$
        VM \cong \pi^*T^*B
    $$
    covering the identity. Now, we let $F^t_{\alpha}$ denote the flow of $X_{\alpha}$ and we will denote by
    $F_{\alpha} := F_{\alpha}^1: M \to M$ the flow at time $1$. This gives a fibrewise action on $M \to B$ as
    the $X_{\alpha}$ are vertical. We get a bundle map
    $$
        T^*B \times_B M \to M,
    $$
    $$(\alpha_b, m) \mapsto F_{\alpha}(m)$$
    and we claim that this is a vector bundle action. Transitivity is clear as $F_{\alpha}$ is a fibrewise diffeomorphism. It remains to show that
    the action is commutative i.e. that the flows of the $X_{\alpha}$ commute. We let $\alpha, \beta \in \Omega^1(B)$ be two one-forms with corresponding
    vector fields $X_{\alpha}, X_{\beta}$.  Then, using the formula from Cartan calculus,
    \begin{align*}
        \iota_{[X_{\alpha}, X_{\beta}]}\omega & = (L_{X_{\alpha}}\iota_{X_{\beta}} - \iota_{X_{\beta}}L_{X_{\alpha}})\omega                             \\
                                              & = -L_{X_{\alpha}}\pi^*\beta - \iota_{X_{\beta}}d\iota_{X_{\alpha}}\omega                                \\
                                              & = -L_{X_{\alpha}}\pi^* {\beta} + \iota_{X_{\beta}}\pi^*d\alpha                                          \\
                                              & = -(d\iota_{X_{\alpha}}\pi^* {\beta} + \iota_{X_{\alpha}}\pi^*d{\beta}) + \iota_{X_{\beta}}\pi^*d\alpha \\
                                              & = 0
    \end{align*}
    since $d\pi (X_{\alpha}) = d\pi(X_{\beta}) = 0$. Now, by non-degeneracy of $\omega$, it follows that $[X_{\alpha}, X_{\beta}] = 0$ for all $\alpha, \beta \in \Omega^1(B)$ and hence the flows commute. We therefore have a vector bundle action of $T^*B \to B$ on $M \to B$ and hence $M \to B$ has the structure of an affine torus bundle.
\end{proof}

It is through this action that the base space of a Lagrangian fibration
inherits an integral affine structure. Indeed the bundle of stabilisers for
this action $\Lambda \subset T^*B$ is a discrete sub-bundle of the cotangent
space and it will turn out that this will locally be spanned by closed one
forms. Since by Proposition \ref{prop:integral-affine-equiv-lattice} this is
equivalent to the base space being an integral affine manifold, this will be
exactly how $B$ inherits an integral affine structure.

We in fact wish to show that $\Lambda$ is a Lagrangian submanifold of $T^*B$.
We will first need the following lemma.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:flow-closed-form}
    Let $\alpha \in \Omega^1(B)$ be a one-form and let $X_{\alpha}$ be its corresponding vector field on $M$ and denote by $F_{\alpha}^t$ its flow.
    Then
    $$
        (F_{\alpha}^t)^*\omega = \omega - t\pi^*d\alpha
    $$
    and so $F_{\alpha}^1$ is a symplectomorphism if and only if $d\alpha=0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    We start by integrating
    \begin{align*}
        \int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(F_{\alpha}^t)^*\omega = (F_{\alpha}^t)^*\omega - \omega.
    \end{align*}
    On the otherhand, calculating this derivative yields
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{\partial}{\partial t}(F_{\alpha}^t)^*\omega & =  (F_{\alpha}^t)^*L_{X_{\alpha}}\omega     \\
                                                          & = (F_{\alpha}^t)^*d\iota_{X_{\alpha}}\omega \\
                                                          & = -(F_{\alpha}^t)^*\pi^* d\alpha            \\
                                                          & = - (\pi \circ F_{\alpha}^t)^*d\alpha       \\
                                                          & = -\pi^*d\alpha.
    \end{align*}
    Now $\int_{0}^{t}-\pi^*d\alpha = -t\pi^*d\alpha$ and so
    \begin{align*}
        (F_{\alpha}^t)^*\omega - \omega & = -t\pi^*d\alpha
    \end{align*}
    as required.
\end{proof}

We can now show the following proposition regarding the bundle of stabilisers
$\Lambda$.

\begin{prop} \label{prop:stabiliser-lagr}
    The lattice $\Lambda \subset T^*B$ is a Lagrangian submanifold.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
    Since by assumption our fibres are compact, by Remark \ref{rem:torus-structure} we must have that $\Lambda_p \cong \Z^n$. Now let $U \subseteq B$ be an open set on the base such that
    $\Lambda|_{U} \cong U \times \Z^n$. Now, the image of any section of $\Lambda|_{U}$ is given by the graph of a one-form $\alpha: B \to T^*B$ such that
    its corresponding time one flow $F_{\alpha}$ is the identity on $\pi^{-1}(U)$. But by Lemma \ref{lemma:flow-closed-form} this implies that $\alpha$ is closed and by
    Lemma \ref{lemma:cotangent-lagrangian-sub} the image of each section is a Lagrangian submanifold of $T^*B$.
\end{proof}

We are now able to see how the base space of a Lagrangian fibration inherits an
integral affine structure.

\begin{corollary} \label{corr:Lag-Fibr-IAS}
    The base space of any Lagrangian fibration $M \to B$ inherits an integral affine structure.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
    By Proposition \ref{prop:stabiliser-lagr}, $\Lambda \subset T^*B$ is a discrete lattice in $T^*B$ locally spanned by closed one-forms. By Proposition \ref{prop:integral-affine-equiv-lattice} this is equivalent to an
    integral affine structure on $B$
\end{proof}
The lattice $\Lambda$ will be an important object of study throughout the remainder of this thesis. We introduce some terminology for it now.
\begin{definition}[Period Lattice]
    Let $B$ be an integral affine manifold and let $\Lambda \subset T^*B$ be the corresponding lattice in the cotangent bundle. Then
    we will call $\Lambda$ the period lattice associated to the integral affine structure on $B$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
    The symplectic form on $T^*B$ descends to $T^*B/\Lambda$ and the projection map $\tau : T^*B/\Lambda \to B$ is a
    Lagrangian fibration.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    The local sections of $\Lambda$ are by definition, given by closed one-forms. Locally, a section of $\Lambda$ looks like $$\alpha = \sum_i m_i dx_i$$
    where $m_i \in \Z$. Two points $x,y \in T_p^*B$ are identified if $x = y + \alpha(p)$ for some local section $\alpha$.

    But this is just a shift by a closed one form on $B$. By Proposition
    \ref{prop:shift-by-closed}, the shift by a closed one-form is a
    symplectomorphism and hence preserves the symplectic form. It follows that the
    symplectic form descends to the quotient. This means that there is a unique
    symplectic form $\tilde{\omega}$ on $T^*B/\Lambda$ such that $$\pi^*
        \tilde{\omega} = \omega$$ where $\pi: T^*B \to T^*B/\Lambda$ is the projection
    map. Since the fibres of $T^*B \to B$ are Lagrangian it follows that the fibres
    of $T^*B/\Lambda \to B$ are too.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    We have thus constructed a torus bundle $\tau : T^*B/\Lambda \to B$ which acts fibrewise on our original Lagrangian fibration $M \to B$. Indeed, this torus bundle is itself a Lagrangian fibration
    and inherits a symplectic form from $T^*B$. The torus bundle
    $\tau : T^*B/\Lambda \to B$ always has a global section, namely the projection of the zero section. However, our original Lagrangian fibration
    $M \to B$ has only the structure of an \emph{affine torus bundle}. It becomes a torus bundle itself only if it admits a global section $\sigma: B \to M$ in which case
    it identifies with the torus bundle $T^*B/\Lambda \to B$. There are in general, obstructions to the existence of such a global section.
\end{remark}

If we assume that the obstructions to the existence of a global section vanish,
then $M \to B$ itself becomes a torus bundle and moreover, we can identify it
with $T^*B/\Lambda \to B$ in a way that respects the group structure.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:identification-of-bundle}
    Let $\sigma: B \to M$ be a global section of $M \to B$ and $\alpha \in \Omega^1(B)$ be a one-form. Then this determines a unique, fibre bundle isomorphism
    $A: T^*B/\Lambda \to M$ that is $T^*B/\Lambda$-equivariant such that the following diagram commutes.
    \[
        \begin{tikzcd}
            T^*B/\Lambda \arrow[r,"A"] \arrow[d,"\hat{F_{\alpha}}"'] & M \arrow[d,"F_{\alpha}"] \\
            T^*B/\Lambda \arrow[r,"A"'] & M
        \end{tikzcd}
    \]
    Here $\hat{F}_{\alpha}$ is determined by the action of $T^*B/\Lambda$ on itself
    (it is namely a Lagrangian fibration over $B$).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    We define $A: T^*B/\Lambda \to M$ by
    \begin{align*}
        A_b: T_b^*B/\Lambda_b \to M_b \\
        [\alpha_b] \mapsto F_{\alpha}(\sigma(b)).
    \end{align*}
    This map is injective as we have quotiented out the fixed point set of this action and hence a bijection by dimension arguments. We saw that this action is a fibrewise diffeomorphism in the
    proof of Theorem \ref{thm:cotangent-action} and so this defines a bundle isomorphism. We now show equivariance. This follows from the following calculation.
    \begin{align*}
        A \circ \hat{F}_{\alpha}([\beta]) & = A([\alpha] + [\beta])                \\
                                          & =F_{\alpha + \beta}(\sigma(b))         \\
                                          & = F_{\alpha}\circ F_{\beta}(\sigma(b)) \\
                                          & = F_{\alpha}\circ A ([\beta])
    \end{align*}
    for $b \in B$ and $\alpha, \beta \in \Omega^1(B)$.
\end{proof}

We note that this gives only a fibrewise, equivariant bundle isomorphism
between $\tau : T^*B/\Lambda$ and $M \to B$. However, we have not yet discussed
when the symplectic structure is preserved. We discuss this now.

\begin{prop}
    The cohomology class $[\sigma^*\omega] \in H^2(B)$ does not depend on the choice of $\sigma$.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
    Since the action of $T^*B$ on $M$ is transitive, any other section $\hat{\sigma}$ differs by an action of $T^*B$ i.e. $\hat{\sigma} = F_{\alpha} \circ \sigma$. It follows that
    \begin{align*}
        \hat{\sigma}^*\omega & = (F_{\alpha} \circ \sigma)^*\omega  \\
                             & = \sigma^*(F_{\alpha}^* \omega)      \\
                             & = \sigma^*(\omega - \pi^*d\alpha)    \\
                             & = \sigma^*\omega - d\alpha. \qedhere
    \end{align*}
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
    Suppose that $\sigma: B \to M$ is a section with $[\sigma^*\omega] = 0 \in H^2(B)$. Then $A: T^*B/\Lambda \to M$ is a symplectomorphism.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    We can assume without loss of generality that $\sigma^*\omega = 0$ as otherwise we can shift it to the zero section by a closed form which is a symplectomorphism. It is enough to show that the map $\hat{A}: T^*B \to M$ covering $A$ is a local symplectomorphism since the symplectic form descends to the quotient.
    The map $\hat{A}$ is determined by the condition
    $$
        \hat{A}\circ \alpha = F_{\alpha} \circ \sigma
    $$
    for every $\alpha \in \Omega^1(B)$. We show that the symplectic form is preserved. We compute the pullback
    \begin{align*}
        \alpha^*\hat{A}^*\omega & = (F_{\alpha} \circ \sigma)^*\omega \\
                                & = \sigma^*F_{\alpha}^* \omega       \\
                                & = \sigma^*(\omega - d\pi^*\alpha)   \\
                                & = -d\sigma^*\pi^*\alpha             \\
                                & = -d\alpha                          \\
                                & = \alpha^*(-d\theta)                \\
                                & = \alpha^* \omega.
    \end{align*}
    Since this holds for any $\alpha \in \Omega^1(B)$ it follows that $\hat{A}^*\omega = \omega$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    We note that the compactness assumption is used so that the flows $F_{\alpha}$ are defined for all time. If we drop the assumption that our fibres are compact and instead
    assume only that the vector fields $X_{\alpha}$ defined in Theorem \ref{thm:cotangent-action} are complete, then most of the above discussion holds with some slight modifications. This
    allows us to obtain the following classification result for regular Lagrangian fibrations with fibre $\R^n$.
\end{remark}
\begin{prop}
    Let $\rho: (E, \omega) \to B$ be a regular Lagrangian fibration with fibre $\R^n$ and suppose that the vector fields $X_{\alpha}$ are complete for all $\alpha \in \Omega^1(B)$. Then $\rho : E \to B$ is
    Lagrangian equivalent to $\pi: (T^*B , \omega_{can} + \rho^*\beta) \to B$ where $\beta$ is a closed two-form on $B$.
    The cohomology class $[\beta] \in H^2(B, \R)$ determines up to Lagrangian equivalence the fibration.
\end{prop}
\begin{proof}
    Since the fibres are contractible, obstruction theory tells us that there is a section $s: B \to E$, for example see \cite{Davis2001LectureNI}. Now, let $\omega$ be the symplectic form
    on $E$ and let $\beta = s^* \omega$. Proposition \ref{prop:shift-by-closed} tells us that $(E, \omega - \rho^*\beta)$ is a Lagrangian bundle. But now
    \begin{align*}
        s^*(\omega - \rho^*\beta) & = s^*\omega - s^*\rho^*s^*\omega^*            \\
                                  & = s^*\omega - (s \circ \rho \circ s)^* \omega \\
                                  & = s^*\omega - s^*\omega                       \\
                                  & = 0
    \end{align*}
    and hence $s$ is a Lagrangian section of $(E, \omega - \rho^*\beta)$. Therefore this section gives a symplectomorphism
    $(T^*B, \omega_{can}) \cong (E, \omega - \rho^*\beta)$ by the same arguments as Lemma \ref{lemma:identification-of-bundle}. Adding the closed two-form $\beta$ to both bundles gives the required result.
    Since $(T^*B, \omega_{can})$ is symplectomorphic to $(T^*B, \omega_{can} + \pi^* \beta)$ if and only if $\beta$ is exact it follows that $[\beta] \in H^2(B, \R)$ determines
    the Lagrangian fibration.
\end{proof}

\subsection{(Global) Action-Angle Coordinates}
In \cite{ActionAngle}, Duistermaat studied when a Lagrangian torus fibration
can globally admit a type of coordinate system called \emph{action-angle
    coordinates}. He was motivated by a class of dynamical systems called
\emph{completely integrable Hamiltonian systems}. Roughly speaking, these are
dynamical systems in which there is sufficient symmetry such that the equations
of motion can be exactly solved. The techniques developed in this paper have
been widely used and expanded upon in the theory of integrable systems. We will
now give an outline of some of the key details.

In this section, we will assume that there exists a global Lagrangian section
$\sigma: B \to M$ and that the cohomology class $[\sigma^*\omega] =0 \in
    H^2(B)$ so that $M\to B$ and $T^*B/\Lambda \to B$ are fibrewise
symplectomorphic unless stated otherwise. If we assume also that the base space
$B$ is simply connected then we have that the linear holonomy representation of
$B$ is trivial and so the group bundle $\Lambda \to B$ is trivial. Fixing a
basepoint $b\in B$ we pick some isomorphism $\Lambda_b \cong \Z^n$ and then for
any other $b'\in B$, we identify $\Lambda_b \cong \Lambda_{b'}$ by picking a
path between them. Since by Corollary \ref{corr:Lag-Fibr-IAS}, $B$ is itself an
integral affine manifold, it hence possesses a flat, torsion free connection
and so we use this connection to parallel transport a basis of $\Lambda_b $ to
a basis of $\Lambda_{b'}$.

Since $B$ is assumed to be simply connected, this gives an isomorphism $$
    \Lambda \cong B \times \Z^n $$ that depends on our choice of base point. Any
two such isomorphisms will differ by an element of $GL(n, \Z)$. Let $\beta_1,
    \dots, \beta_n$ be a global frame for $B \times \Z^n$. We note that the
$\beta_i$ will be closed since sections of $\Lambda \subset T^*B$ are given by
closed one-forms. Since $\Lambda$ is a full rank lattice, this also defines a
global frame for $T^*B$ and hence we have trivializations

$$
    T^*B \cong B \times \R^n
$$
and
$$
    M \cong T^*B/\Lambda \cong B \times (\R/\Z)^n.
$$

\begin{definition}
    We define \emph{angle coordinates} on $M \cong B \times (\R/\Z)^n$ by
    $$
        s : M \cong B \times (\R/\Z)^n \to (\R/\Z)^n
    $$
    where $s$ is the projection map.
\end{definition}

Recall that we can associate vector fields on $M$ to the $\beta_i \in
    \Omega^1(B)$ by $\iota_{X_{\beta_i}} \omega = -\pi^* \beta_i$ and a quick
calculation using Cartan's formula shows that these vector fields are
symplectic i.e. $L_{X_{\beta_i}}\omega = 0$ since both $\beta_i$ and $\omega$
are closed. Denoting their corresponding flow by $F_i^t$, in terms of the angle
coordinates we have that

$$
    s_j(F_i^t(x)) =\begin{cases*}
        s_j(x), \quad i \neq j, \\
        s_j(x) + t, \quad i = j
    \end{cases*}
$$
for $x \in M$. Indeed, a point $x \in M$ identifies with
$$
    x = (p, a_1 \beta_1(p), \dots , a_n \beta_n(p))
$$
under $M \cong B \times \R^n$ where $p \in B$ and $a_i \in \R$. The flows of $X_{\beta_i}$ preserve the fibres and are linear maps. Hence, the flow of $\beta_i$
acts on $x$ by
$$
    F_i^t(x) = (p, a_1 \beta_1 ,  \dots a_i \beta_i(p) + t \beta_i(p), \dots , a_n \beta_n(p))
$$
and since $s_i$ is projection onto the $i^{th}$ component, the result follows.

Moreover, since $B$ is simply connected the $\beta_i$ are in fact exact i.e.
$\beta_i = dI_i$. Since the $\beta_i$ form a global frame, $dI_p = (dI_1, \dots
    , dI_n)_p$ is full rank at each $p \in M$ and hence we can use them to define
coordinates on the base.

\begin{definition}
    We define \emph{action-angle coordinates} on $M$ by $(\pi^*I_1, \dots , \pi^* I_n, s_1, \dots , s_n)$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
    It is clear from the above discussion that the action coordinates $I_i$ define an integral affine structure in $B$. Indeed, the differentials
    of these span a full-rank lattice in $T^*B$ and hence by Proposition \ref{prop:integral-affine-equiv-lattice} they equip $B$ with an integral affine structure.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
    In these action-angle coordinates we can write the symplectic form as $ \omega
        = \sum_i dI_i \wedge ds_i. $
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
    For a regular Lagrangian torus fibration $M \to B$, even if there does not exist global action-angle coordinates, we can cover $B$ in
    charts such that in each chart the fibration does admit global action-angle coordinates.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Completely integrable Hamiltonian systems}
We will give a very brief introduction to completely integrable Hamiltonian
systems as historically, they were important in the development of much of this
theory and they also provide us with many examples of Lagrangian torus
fibrations.

\begin{definition}
    Let  $(M^{2n}, \omega)$ be a symplectic manifold. A \emph{completely integrable Hamiltonian
        system} $(M, \omega, F)$, is a map
    $$
        F=(f_1, \dots , f_n) : M \to \R^n
    $$
    such that the components satisfy
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\{f_i, f_j\} = 0$ for all $i,j$.
        \item $df_1 \wedge \dots \wedge df_n \neq 0$ on an open, dense set of $M$.
    \end{enumerate}

\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
    The bracket $\{\cdot , \cdot\}$ is the \emph{Poisson} bracket on $M$. It is defined as
    $$
        \{f,g\} = \omega(X_f, X_g)
    $$
    with $f,g \in C^{\infty}(M)$.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
    The condition $\{f_i, f_j\} \neq 0$ ensures that the fibres of the map $F$ are Langrangian submanifolds of $M$. The second condition
    is a regularity condition on the fibres of $F$.
\end{remark}

The following theorem shows the relationship between completely integrable
Hamiltonian systems and Lagrangian fibrations.

\begin{theorem}[Liouville-Arnold]
    Let $(M, \omega, F)$ be a completely integrable Hamiltonian system. Suppose that $x \in \R^n$ is a regular value of $F$ and that
    $F^{-1}(x)$ is compact and connected. Then there exists an open neighborhood $U \subset \R^n$ of $x$ such that
    $$
        F|_{F^{-1}(U)} : M \to U
    $$
    is a regular Lagrangian torus fibration.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}
    This allows us to associate a Lagrangian fibration to an open neighborhood of a regular fibre of the completely integrable Hamiltonian system. Conversely, given
    a Lagrangian fibration admitting global action-angle coordinates, the action coordinates determine a completely integrable Hamiltonian system.
\end{remark}
